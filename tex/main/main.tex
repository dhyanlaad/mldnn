\title{A Physics-Informed Functional Link Neural Network based on M\"untz--Legendre Polynomials for Non-linear Stochastic Fractional Differential Equations}
\date{}
\author{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study the numerical solution of nonlinear Caputo stochastic fractional
differential equations of order $\alpha\in(1/2,1]$ driven by multiplicative
white noise. The schemes available for this class are mesh-based time
integrators, whose convergence order is capped by the regularity of the
solution paths and whose nonlocal memory term must be rebuilt at every step.
We propose a M\"untz--Legendre physics-informed functional link neural network
(ML-PIFLNN) that approximates the solution globally on the whole interval.
The time input passes through a fractional feature layer of orthogonalized
M\"untz--Legendre polynomials, so that the output is a direct linear
combination of the features with no hidden layers, and both fractional
integrals are evaluated through operational matrices assembled once before
training. Because a globally fitted diffusion coefficient is anticipating,
the stochastic operational matrix reproduces a Skorokhod integral rather than
the It\^o integral the model calls for. We compute the resulting Malliavin
trace in closed finite-dimensional form and remove it in a second, corrected
pass. Affine problems are then solved by a single Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse,
and nonlinear problems by damped Gauss--Newton collocation. We prove a
mean-square error bound that separates the optimizer, collocation, stochastic
projection, Malliavin consistency, and frozen-trace contributions, and we
validate the method numerically against high-resolution Milstein and fast
fractional Euler--Maruyama reference solutions on four benchmark problems. The
observed convergence order in the spectral degree matches the
$\alpha-1/2$ Hölder regularity of the driving stochastic convolution,
the same order that caps mesh-based schemes in the number of time steps. We
discuss this rate, rather than claiming to exceed it, in
\S\ref{sec:numerics}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In this paper, we study the following nonlinear Caputo stochastic fractional differential equation (CSFDE):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cfsde}
    \DD y(t) = b\bigl(t,y(t)\bigr) + \sigma\bigl(t,y(t)\bigr)\,\sdv,
    \qquad t\in(0,1],\qquad y(0)=y_0,
\end{equation}
where $y \colon \Omega\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ is a stochastic process, $b:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ and $\sigma:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ are measurable drift and diffusion coefficients respectively, $\DD y(t)$ is the Caputo fractional derivative of order
$\alpha\in(1/2,1]$,
$B_t$ denotes a standard scalar Brownian motion defined on a complete
filtered probability space
$(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t\in[0,1]},\PP)$,
$\sigma(t,y(t))\,\sdv$ is the multiplicative white noise term, and
$y_0$ is an $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable initial value with
$\EE[y_0^2]<\infty$. Equations of the form~\eqref{eq:cfsde} combine a fractional operator carrying a weakly singular memory kernel with a stochastic forcing term, and it is the interaction of the two that gives rise to the numerical difficulties addressed in this work.

Stochastic differential equations are widely used to model dynamic systems
across various scientific fields and have real-world applications in
mathematical finance, control theory, physics, and population dynamics, all
supported by an established mathematical
framework~\cite{oksendal2003stochastic}. When the phenomenon under study exhibits
memory or hereditary behaviour, the integer-order derivative is replaced by a
fractional one: the Caputo derivative encodes the entire history of the state
through its weakly singular kernel, a feature that integer-order models cannot
reproduce~\cite{podlubny1999}. Fractional stochastic differential equations
(FSDEs) of the form~\eqref{eq:cfsde} accordingly arise wherever long memory and
noise act together, as in the pricing of financial assets driven by long-memory
noise~\cite{tien2013}. That same memory dependence is what makes them hard to
solve. Exact solutions are available only for a narrow class of linear problems,
and in most cases the analytical solution of an FSDE cannot be derived in closed
form, so reliable numerical methods for FSDEs have received considerable
attention in recent years.

Up to now, various numerical schemes have been constructed for FSDEs.
\cite{kamrani2015} studied a class of FSDEs driven by additive noise, applied
a Galerkin method based on Jacobi polynomials, and proved the convergence of
the proposed method. \cite{ahmadi2017} introduced a collocation method based
on Gaussian radial basis functions to approximate the solution of FSDEs, and
established the existence and uniqueness of the approximation. Later,
\cite{doan2020euler} presented an Euler--Maruyama (EM) method for a class of
FSDEs driven by multiplicative white noise with Caputo fractional order
$\alpha\in(1/2,1)$, and proved its strong convergence with order
$\alpha-1/2$ when the coefficients are independent of time. The
well-posedness and the regularity of the solution paths of such Caputo FSDEs in
$L^p$ spaces were established by \cite{huong2023lp}. EM-type methods, however,
require a large computational cost, especially for small step sizes, because of
the historical dependence and nonlocal property of the fractional derivative:
every step must revisit the entire solution history, so a direct EM method
requires $O(n^2)$ work for $n$ time steps. To overcome this difficulty,
\cite{zhang2023fast} constructed a fast EM method by approximating the singular
kernel with the sum-of-exponentials (SOE) technique of \cite{jiang2017}, which
reduces the computational cost to $O(n N_{\mathrm{exp}})$ while retaining the
strong convergence order $\alpha-1/2$. The fast EM method is among the
most efficient schemes currently available for this class of equations, and both
it and the direct EM method serve as reference methods in what follows.

Despite their differences, all of the above methods share one structural
feature: they are mesh-based, step-by-step time integrators. Their accuracy is
tied to the temporal mesh, and their strong convergence order is limited to
$\alpha-1/2$, which is below one half for every $\alpha\in(1/2,1)$.
This limit is imposed by the regularity of the solution paths themselves rather
than by any deficiency of the schemes, so acceleration techniques such as the
SOE approximation reduce the computational cost without improving the
convergence order. A different approach is to approximate the solution globally,
using a basis adapted to the equation instead of a time-stepping grid. For
deterministic fractional differential equations, spectral and
operational-matrix methods based on M\"untz--Legendre polynomials are well
suited to this task, since the basis contains the fractional powers
$t^{k\alpha}$ that appear in the solutions of Caputo-type
equations~\cite{mokhtary2016}. For stochastic problems, M\"untz--Legendre
polynomials have been applied to stochastic fractional integro-differential
equations by \cite{singh2022variableorder,singhmehra2023parameter}, and a fractional quadrature rule that is
exact on the M\"untz--Legendre scaling functions was constructed by
\cite{kumari2025fractional}. In a related but different direction, Genocchi
polynomials and Genocchi wavelets have been used to build operational matrices
for fractional differential equations by \cite{isah2016genocchi}, and a deep
neural network based on fractional-order Genocchi functions has been proposed
by \cite{rahimkhani2023numerical} for stochastic differential equations driven
by fractional Brownian motion. A different established route to a global
representation of a stochastic solution is the Wiener chaos (polynomial chaos)
expansion, which represents the solution as a series in an orthogonal basis of
the driving noise itself, with deterministic, realization-independent
coefficients~\cite{cameronmartin1947,houluorozovskiizhou2006}. Because the
expansion coefficients there do not depend on the realized Brownian path, such
schemes do not encounter the anticipativity difficulty described next. We do
not pursue this route because it expands in the noise rather than in time,
which does not by itself supply the fractional-order operational calculus of
\S\ref{sec:proposed_method}, but the contrast is instructive.

Global polynomial approximation schemes for stochastic models, however, face a
difficulty of their own under highly nonlinear multiplicative noise. This is
not a difficulty specific to M\"untz--Legendre or Genocchi bases: the same
non-adapted-coefficient issue arises throughout the broader family of
stochastic operational-matrix methods for It\^o--Volterra integral equations
built on block-pulse and related deterministic function
bases~\cite{maleknejad2012}, where it is typically not addressed at all, and
the fitted stochastic integral is used as though it were already adapted.
Since the globally fitted coefficients depend on the realized Brownian trajectory, the
resulting projected integrands need not be adapted, and the stochastic
projection cannot in general be identified with the adapted It\^o integral
without an additional anticipative correction. Omitting the corresponding
Malliavin trace therefore leaves a systematic bias away from the desired It\^o
solution. In addition, existing numerical implementations in the literature
recompute the fractional integrals by numerical quadrature whenever the
approximation is updated, so the cost of evaluating the weakly singular kernel
is paid continually. To the best of our knowledge, no global nonlinear solver
for the CSFDE~\eqref{eq:cfsde} driven by multiplicative white noise both maps
the two fractional integrals into static operational matrices via a Fubini
contraction and computes and subtracts the exact Malliavin trace correction.

In this work, we introduce the M\"untz--Legendre physics-informed functional
link neural network (ML-PIFLNN) method to solve~\eqref{eq:cfsde}. Applying the
Riemann--Liouville fractional integral to both sides of~\eqref{eq:cfsde} gives
an equivalent stochastic Volterra integral equation, whose solution is
approximated globally on $[0,1]$. The time input passes through a fractional
feature layer of orthogonalized M\"untz--Legendre polynomials with exponents
$\{k\alpha : k=0,1,\dots,n\}$. In contrast to deep neural networks, the
architecture carries no hidden layers, and the state, drift, and diffusion terms
are projected onto the same functional space, so that both fractional integrals
reduce to algebra against two fixed operational matrices. The deterministic
Riemann--Liouville matrix is exact and available in closed
form~\cite{kumari2025fractional}. The stochastic matrix is assembled once, prior
to training, as a stochastic Fubini adjoint contraction tensor, which projects
the weakly singular It\^o integrals onto the basis and evaluates them across a
large batch of sample paths in a single contraction, with no numerical
quadrature during training. Because the network is linear in its weights,
affine problems are solved directly by a Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse solve,
while fully nonlinear problems are solved by damped Gauss--Newton collocation.
In both cases the fitted diffusion coefficient is anticipating. We compute the
Malliavin trace this induces in closed finite-dimensional form and subtract it
in a second, corrected pass. This single correction reduces, but does not by
itself asymptotically eliminate, the resulting bias. \S\ref{sec:convergence}
shows that iterating it to a fixed point would restore a genuine convergence
guarantee, provided a stated contraction hypothesis holds, which we have not
verified in this revision.

The outline of this paper is as follows. Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries}
introduces the fractional operators, the M\"untz--Legendre polynomials, and the
well-posedness of the model problem. Section~\ref{sec:proposed_method}
formulates the ML-PIFLNN method and constructs the two operational matrices.
Section~\ref{sec:malliavin} develops the Malliavin calculus behind the
anticipative correction and derives the trace explicitly.
Section~\ref{sec:convergence} provides the convergence analysis of the scheme,
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} reports numerical experiments on four benchmark
problems of increasing complexity, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes
with a summary of what is established versus assumed.

\subsection{Model problem}\label{sec:model_problem}
Let $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)$ be a complete probability space, $T = [0, 1]$, and $\{\mathcal F_t\}_{t \in T} \subseteq \mathcal F$ be a filtration satisfying the usual conditions. Let $\{B_t\}_{t \in T}$ be a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion on the probability space adapted to the filtration. Consider the formal Caputo stochastic initial value problem (CSIVP) of order $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$ for a stochastic process $y : T \times \Omega \to \mathbb R$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:csivp}
    \DD y(t) = b(t, y(t)) + \sigma(t, y(t))\sdv, \quad y(0) = y_0,
\end{equation}
where $dB_t/dt$ denotes white noise. For the case of $\alpha = 1$, we recover a standard nonlinear stochastic initial value problem:
\begin{equation}
    \dd{y(t)} = b(t, y(t))\dd{t} + \sigma(t, y(t))\dd{B_t}, \quad y(0) = y_0.
\end{equation}
We reformulate the formal CSIVP~\eqref{eq:csivp} into a mathematically rigorous stochastic Volterra integral equation (SVIE) and impose assumptions on the functions $b$, $\sigma$, and $y_0$ in \S~\ref{sec:well_posedness}.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:preliminaries}
In this section, we introduce the fractional operators, the M\"untz--Legendre polynomials, and the concepts of a physics-informed neural network (PINN) and a functional link neural network (FLNN) as the machine learning frameworks we utilize. We introduce and develop the requisite Malliavin calculus in \S\ref{sec:malliavin}.

\begin{definition}\label{def:caputo}
    Let $\alpha > 0$, $n = \lceil \alpha \rceil$, and $f \in \mathcal{AC}^n([0, 1])$. Let $\Gamma$ denote the gamma function. The \emph{left-sided Caputo fractional derivative} of order $\alpha$ of $f$ is
    \begin{equation}
        \DD f(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(n - \alpha)} \int_0^t (t - s)^{n - \alpha - 1}f^{(n)}(s)\dd{s}.
    \end{equation} 
\end{definition}

For $\alpha \in (1/2, 1)$, the Caputo derivative of $f$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:caputo_explicit}
    \DD f(t) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(1 - \alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{-\alpha}f'(s)\dd{s}.
\end{equation}
This formula degenerates at $\alpha=1$, since $\Gamma(1-\alpha)\to\Gamma(0)=\infty$. At $\alpha=1$ the Caputo derivative reduces instead to the classical derivative, $\DD[][1] f(t) = f'(t)$, directly from Definition~\ref{def:caputo} with $n=1$. We use \eqref{eq:caputo_explicit} for $\alpha\in(1/2,1)$ and this classical identity at $\alpha=1$ throughout.

\begin{definition}
    Let $\alpha > 0$ and $f \in \mathcal L^1([0, 1])$. The \emph{left-sided Riemann--Liouville fractional integral} of order $\alpha$ of $f$ is
    \begin{equation}
        \II f(t) = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}f(s)\dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

We shall use the fact that for $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$ and $f \in \mathcal{AC}([0, 1])$,
\begin{equation}
    \II (\DD f(t)) = f(t) - f(0),
\end{equation}
We do not attempt to extend this identity, or the Caputo derivative $\DD$ itself, to stochastic processes: the CSIVP~\eqref{eq:csivp} is only a formal expression, since $\DD y(t)$ is not defined pathwise for a process $y$ with rough (non-absolutely-continuous) sample paths. Instead, applying $\II$ formally to \eqref{eq:csivp} and using this identity motivates the stochastic Volterra integral equation~\eqref{eq:svie} below, which is the rigorous object we work with throughout: a stochastic process $y$ \emph{solves} the CSIVP precisely when it satisfies \eqref{eq:svie}, and \eqref{eq:svie} is the definition we adopt.

It is convenient throughout to carry the normalising factor $1/\Gamma(\alpha)$ in the kernel itself. We therefore write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
    K_\alpha(t, s) = \frac{(t - s)^{\alpha - 1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\mathbf 1_{\{0 \leq s < t\}}, \quad (t, s) \in T \times T,
\end{equation}
so that for $f \in \mathcal L^1([0, 1])$ and an adapted, square-integrable process $u$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel_operators}
    \II f(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)f(s)\dd{s}, \quad \JJ u(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)u(s)\dd{B_s},
\end{equation}
the operator $\JJ$ being the stochastic counterpart of $\II$. We reserve $\dd{B_s}$ for the It\^o integral, and write $\delta B_s$ for its anticipating extension, which we introduce in \S\ref{sec:malliavin}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:kernel_l2}
    For $\alpha > 1/2$, the fractional kernel satisfies
    \begin{equation}
        \int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \dd{s} = \frac{t^{2\alpha-1}}{(2\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha)^2} < \infty,
    \end{equation}
    which is precisely the condition needed for the stochastic convolution against the kernel to be well defined in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$. This inequality resurfaces in \S\ref{sec:malliavin} to justify the regularity of the fractional stochastic operational matrices.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Well-posedness of the model problem}\label{sec:well_posedness}
We start by developing the necessary stochastic framework. Consider the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)$ and filtration from \S~\ref{sec:model_problem}. For any $\mathcal F$-measurable real random variable $X : \Omega \to \mathbb R$, we define its mathematical expectation
\begin{equation}
    \EE (X) = \int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\PP(\dd{\omega}).
\end{equation}
We further define the Hilbert space of all equivalence classes of square integrable random variables on $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP) = \{X : \Omega \to \mathbb R \mid \text{$X$ is $\mathcal F$-measurable and $\EE(X^2) < \infty$}\}.
\end{equation}
This space is equipped with a standard inner product
\begin{equation}
    \ip{u, v}_{\mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)} = \EE(uv) = \int_{\Omega}u(\omega)v(\omega)\PP(\dd{\omega})
\end{equation}
inducing the norm
\begin{equation}
    \norm{u}_{\mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)} = \ip{u, u}_{\mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)}^{1/2} = \EE (u^2)^{1/2}
\end{equation}
for all $u, v \in \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)$. For time dependent analysis over $T = [0, 1]$, we consider the Banach space of continuous functions from $T$ into $\mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F_t, \PP)$, which we denote by $\mathcal C(T; \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP))$. We say that a stochastic process $y : T \times \Omega \to \mathbb R$ belongs to this space if
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item $y(t, \cdot) \in \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F_t, \PP)$ for all $t \in T$, and
    \item the mapping $t \mapsto y(t, \cdot)$ is continuous in the mean-square sense for all $t \in T$:
    \begin{equation}
        \lim_{s \to t} \norm{y(t, \cdot) - y(s, \cdot)}_{\mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)} = 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The space $\mathcal C(T; \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP))$ is endowed with the uniform norm:
\begin{equation}
    \norm{y}_{\mathcal C(T; \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP))} = \sup_{t \in T} \norm{y(t)}_{\mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)} = \sup_{t \in T} \EE(y(t)^2)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}

We now formally integrate the CSIVP~\eqref{eq:csivp} with the operator $\II$ to get a SVIE:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:svie}
    y(t) = y_0 + \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}b(s, y(s))\dd{s} + \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}\sigma(s, y(s))\dd{B_s}.
\end{equation}
For a stochastic process $y$ to solve the CSIVP~\eqref{eq:csivp}, we mean for it to satisfy the SVIE~\eqref{eq:svie}. We now establish assumptions on $b$, $\sigma$, and $y_0$ to ensure the SVIE~\eqref{eq:svie} is well-posed.
\begin{enumerate}[series=assumptions,label=(A\arabic*)]
    \item \label{as:a1} $y_0 \in \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F_0, \PP)$.
    \item \label{as:a2} The drift $b : T \times \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ and diffusion $\sigma : T \times \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ are Borel measurable and there exists a constant $L > 0$ such that for all $t \in T$ and $y_1, y_2 \in \mathbb R$,
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{b(t, y_1) - b(t, y_2)} + \abs{\sigma(t, y_1) - \sigma(t, y_2)} \leq L\abs{y_1 - y_2}.
    \end{equation}
    \item \label{as:a3} There exists a constant $C > 0$ such that for $t \in T$ and $y \in \mathbb R$, it holds that
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{b(t, y)}^2 + \abs{\sigma(t, y)}^2 \leq C(1 + \abs{y}^2).
    \end{equation}
    Assumption~\ref{as:a3} is in fact implied by Assumption~\ref{as:a2} together with $\sup_{t\in T}(\abs{b(t,0)}+\abs{\sigma(t,0)})<\infty$ (immediate from the Lipschitz bound with $y_2=0$ and the triangle inequality). We retain it as a separate, named hypothesis purely for convenient reference in the sequel.
\end{enumerate}
Assumptions \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a3} are the only hypotheses used for well-posedness (Theorem~\ref{thm:well_posed} below). Assumptions~\ref{as:a4}--\ref{as:a7} are introduced later, in \S\ref{sec:proposed_method} and \S\ref{sec:convergence}, as they are needed. We flag one qualitative distinction: \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a4} are hypotheses on the \emph{data} of the problem ($b$, $\sigma$, $y_0$), verifiable in principle by inspecting the model equation, whereas \ref{as:a5}--\ref{as:a7} are hypotheses on the \emph{algorithm's own output} (the training Hessian, the fitted coefficients, and their Malliavin derivatives) and are consequently a qualitatively stronger and less directly checkable kind of assumption.

\begin{theorem}[Well-posedness]\label{thm:well_posed}
    Under Assumptions~\ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a3}, the SVIE~\eqref{eq:svie} admits a unique solution $y \in \mathcal C(T; \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP))$.
\end{theorem}
We do not reprove this here. It is a standard Banach fixed-point argument for weakly singular Volterra equations with Lipschitz, linear-growth coefficients, entirely analogous to the Picard iteration we use for a different purpose in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:mall_dev_y}, and well-posedness and $\mathcal L^p$ path regularity in this Caputo fractional stochastic setting are established by \cite{huong2023lp}.

\subsection{M\"untz--Legendre polynomials}\label{sec:muntz_legendre}
The architecture of our machine learning framework is heavily dependent on the M\"untz--Legendre polynomials \cite{milovanovic1999muntz}, which possess complex degrees (and by extension, fractional degrees), better capturing the singular behaviour of solutions to fractional differential equations. Define a M\"untz sequence $\Lambda = \{\lambda_n\}$ to be a set of complex numbers with $\Re (\lambda_k) > -1/2$ for all $k \in \mathbb N_0$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:muntz_legendre}
    For a M\"untz sequence $\Lambda$, the $m$th degree M\"untz--Legendre polynomial on $[0, 1]$ is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        M^{\Lambda}_m(t) = \sum_{k=0}^m \eta^{(m)}_k t^{\lambda_k}, \quad \eta^{(m)}_k = \prod_{j=0}^{m-1} (\lambda_j + \overline{\lambda_m} + 1) \prod_{\substack{j=0 \\ j \neq k}}^m \frac{1}{\lambda_k - \lambda_j}
    \end{equation} 
\end{definition}
Additionally, the polynomials possess the orthogonality property
\begin{equation}\label{eq:muntz_orthogonality}
    \int_0^1 M^{\Lambda}_j(t)\overline{M^\Lambda_k(t)}\dd{t} = \frac{\delta_{j,k}}{2\Re (\lambda_j) + 1}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{j,k}$ is the Kronecker delta function.

To guarantee that the M\"untz--Legendre system $\{M_0^\Lambda(t), M_1^\Lambda(t), \dots\}$ forms a complete basis in $\mathcal L^2(0, 1)$, the sequence $\Lambda = \{\lambda_n\}$ must satisfy the complex M\"untz--Sz\'{a}sz condition \cite{szasz1916}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:muntz_szasz_completeness}
Let $\Lambda = \{\lambda_n\}$ be a M\"untz sequence. The system $\{M_0^\Lambda(t), M_1^\Lambda(t), \dots\}$ is complete in $\mathcal L^2(0, 1)$ if and only if the series
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1 + 2\Re (\lambda_k)}{1 + \abs{\lambda_k}^2}
    \label{eq:muntz_szasz}
\end{equation}
diverges to infinity. Under this condition, any function $f \in \mathcal L^2(0, 1)$ admits the expansion
\begin{equation}
    f(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k M_k^\Lambda(t), \quad c_k = (2\Re(\lambda_k) + 1)\int_0^1 f(t)\overline{M_k^\Lambda(t)} \dd{t}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

In the ensuing analysis, we fix $\Lambda = \{n\alpha\}$. In this case, the series \eqref{eq:muntz_szasz} reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1 + 2k\alpha}{1 + (k\alpha)^2} \sim \frac{2}{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{k}
\end{equation}
which establishes the completeness of the basis for any fractional order $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$.

We may also represent the M\"untz--Legendre polynomials for this choice of $\Lambda$ as classical shifted Jacobi polynomials \cite{HOSSEINPOUR2019344}, up to the normalizing constant $\eta_m^{(m)}$ of Definition~\ref{def:muntz_legendre} (the two sides agree only up to this leading coefficient, since $P_m^{(0,-1+1/\alpha)}$ is normalized by the classical Jacobi convention, not by the M\"untz--Legendre orthogonality~\eqref{eq:muntz_orthogonality}):
\begin{equation}
    M_m^\Lambda(t) \propto P_m^{(0, -1 + 1/\alpha)}(2t^\alpha - 1).
\end{equation}
This enables the usage of the standard three term recurrence relation of Jacobi polynomials to stably compute higher order M\"untz--Legendre polynomials up to a scalar rescaling.

\subsection{Functional link neural networks}
Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) embed a governing differential equation directly into the loss function of a neural network, so that the trained network acts as a mesh-free continuous-time solver rather than a discrete time-stepping scheme. Standard deep PINN architectures, however, rely on nested compositions of weights, biases, and non-linear activation functions, which yield highly non-convex optimization landscapes, and the smooth activation functions typically used struggle to represent the characteristic $t^\alpha$ singularity present in solutions of Caputo-type equations without substantial architectural depth or grid refinement.

To avoid these difficulties we instead use a functional link neural network (FLNN). An FLNN is a single-layer architecture with no hidden layers that replaces depth with a fixed, higher-order functional expansion of the input. Rather than learning non-linearities through composition, an FLNN applies a predetermined functional map $\boldsymbol \Phi$ to the input variable $t$, projecting it into a higher-dimensional feature space, and the network output is then a direct linear combination of these functionally linked features:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal N(t) = \mathbf c^\top \boldsymbol \Phi(t),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf c$ is the trainable weight vector and $\boldsymbol \Phi(t)$ is the fixed feature map. Because the map $\boldsymbol \Phi$ is fixed rather than learned, the network output is linear in $\mathbf c$, and no hidden layers or backpropagation through a composition of activations are required. This architecture coincides structurally with the extreme learning machine (ELM) paradigm, in which a fixed (random or otherwise predetermined) hidden-to-output feature map is combined with a linear, least-squares-trained output layer~\cite{dwivedisrinivasan2020,dongli2021}. Our contribution lies in the choice of feature map and the fractional operational-matrix and Malliavin-correction machinery built on top of it (\S\ref{sec:proposed_method}--\S\ref{sec:malliavin}), not in the linear-output-layer idea itself, which is well established in that literature.

The advantage of the FLNN architecture for the CSFDE~\eqref{eq:cfsde} is that choosing the feature map $\boldsymbol \Phi = \mathbf M_n^\Lambda$ to be the M\"untz--Legendre basis vector of \S\ref{sec:muntz_legendre} embeds the fractional singularity $t^{k\alpha}$ directly into the network architecture, rather than requiring it to be approximated by compositions of smooth activations. Since $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda$ also admits the closed-form deterministic operational matrix and the stochastic operational matrix constructed in \S\ref{sec:proposed_method}, the fractional and stochastic integral operators in the SVIE~\eqref{eq:svie} act on the network output algebraically, without numerical quadrature. Because the network is linear in its weights, training reduces to the least-squares problem of \S\ref{sec:collocation_loss}, admitting an instantaneous closed-form solution in the affine case and a well-conditioned Gauss--Newton iteration in the non-linear case.

\section{Proposed method}\label{sec:proposed_method}
% Verified directly against the full text of Singh \& Mehra \cite{singhmehra2023parameter} (J. Appl. Math. Comput. 69(3), 2675--2694, 2023).
Closest in spirit to the present method is the M\"untz--Legendre collocation scheme of Singh and Mehra~\cite{singhmehra2023parameter}, who derive deterministic and stochastic operational matrices for M\"untz--Legendre polynomials and use them, with a differential-evolution search over the polynomial's free parameter, to solve $D^\alpha_{0,t} u(t) = f(t) + \int_0^t k_1(s,t)u(s)\,ds + \int_0^t k_2(s,t)u(s)\,dW(s)$. Since both kernels depend on $(s,t)$ alone, not on $u$, their collocation system is linear by construction and does not extend to a diffusion coefficient depending non-linearly on $u$, the setting treated here. Nor does their derivation involve a Malliavin derivative, Skorokhod integral, or anticipativity correction. Among their numerical examples, the only case combining a non-zero fractional order with genuine multiplicative noise (their Example 4, $\alpha = 0.75$) is validated only against a mean trajectory and confidence band, not a closed-form solution. An exact-solution check appears only for that example's noise-free reduction or their $\alpha = 0$ stochastic example. The Malliavin trace correction of \S~\ref{sec:malliavin} is accordingly unneeded in their linear, $u$-independent-kernel setting, but becomes necessary once the diffusion depends on the solution itself, as here.
% NOTE: consider moving this positioning paragraph to the introduction/related-work section instead of here.

We formulate the proposed method in this section. To overcome the difficulties of numerically differentiating the rough sample paths of a stochastic process, properly handle non-linear multiplicative noise with the Malliavin correction, and eliminating non-local memory accumulation during training, we propose a unified framework utilizing operational matrices and a physics-informed functional link neural network based on M\"untz--Legendre polynomials (ML-PIFLNN) capable of handling fully coupled non-linear CSIVPs.

Consider the SVIE \eqref{eq:svie}:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = y_0 + \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}b(s, y(s))\dd{s} + \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}\sigma(s, y(s))\dd{B_s},
\end{equation}
and the $(n+1)$-dimensional basis vector of M\"untz--Legendre polynomials with M\"untz sequence $\Lambda$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf M^\Lambda_n(t) = [M_0^\Lambda(t) \; \cdots \; M^\Lambda_n(t)]^\top \in \mathbb R^{n+1}.
\end{equation}

For a realized Brownian sample path $B_t(\omega)$, we approximate the sample path $y(t)$ by projecting it onto $\mathbf M^\Lambda_n(t)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:y_approx}
    y(t) \approx y_n(t) = \mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda (t) = \sum_{k=0}^n c_kM_k^\Lambda(t),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf c \in \mathbb R^{n+1}$ is the primary trainable weight vector to be determined by the ML-PIFLNN. To avoid iteratively performing numerical quadratures over non-linear functions during training, we also project the drift $b$ and diffusion $\sigma$ onto the same basis:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phy_approx}
    b(t, y_n(t)) \approx \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t), \quad \sigma(t, y_n(t)) \approx \mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf M^\Lambda_n(t),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf c_b, \mathbf c_\sigma \in \mathbb R^{n+1}$ are the auxiliary trainable weight vectors. Since $\lambda_0=0$ for our choice of $\Lambda$, $M_0^\Lambda(t) = 1$ for all $t \in T$ (a normalization, not an approximation), so with $\mathbf e = [1 \; 0 \cdots \; 0]^\top \in \mathbb R^{n+1}$ the initial condition admits the \emph{exact} representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:y0_exact}
    y_0 = y_0\mathbf e^\top \mathbf M^\Lambda_n(t) \qquad \text{for every } t \in T,
\end{equation}
which we use, rather than a projection of $y_0$, throughout.

We interpret the deterministic Riemann--Liouville fractional integral as an operator exact for the M\"untz--Legendre basis vector $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:det_approx_matrix}
    \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha-1}b(s, y(s))\dd{s} = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha-1} \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s} = \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf P_\alpha(t) \mathbf M_n^\Lambda (t).
\end{equation}

We similarly employ an operational matrix to approximate the stochastic integral. However, due to the nature of the solver, the coefficient vectors $\mathbf c$, $\mathbf c_b$, and $\mathbf c_\sigma$ are anticipative random variables \cite{NualartPardoux1988}, that is to say, they not adapted to the filtration that the Brownian motion is adapted to. As a result, the operational matrix converges to the Skorokhod integral \cite{skorokhod1975}, an extension of It\^o integrals to non-adapted integrands. This prevents us from seamlessly factoring out the coefficient vector $\mathbf c_\sigma$ and introduces a time-continuous correction term $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma]$:
\begin{equation}\label{stoch_approx_m}
    \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\sigma(s, y(s))\dd{B_s} \approx \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \, \delta B_s \approx \mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma](t),
\end{equation}
where the correction is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_intro}
    \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma](t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(D_s \mathbf c_\sigma)^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s},
\end{equation}
and the operator $D$ denotes the Malliavin derivative. We write the correction as a functional of the coefficient vector it is built from, rather than as a bare function of $t$, because the two passes described below evaluate it at two different vectors, and the discrepancy between the two is a genuine error term that we quantify in \S\ref{sec:convergence}.

Figure~\ref{fig:trace_correction_demo} illustrates the effect of $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma]$ directly, on a single Brownian realization of the geometric Brownian motion~\eqref{eq:gbm} at $\alpha = 1.0$ with $\mu = 0.3$, $\sigma = 0.15$, $y_0 = 1.0$, and $n = 32$: the left panel is the ML-PIFLNN solution $\hat y_n$ produced by the full two-pass scheme with the trace correction applied, and the right panel is the solution obtained by omitting it, i.e.\ reporting the uncorrected first pass $y_n = \mathbf c^{*\top}\mathbf M_n^\Lambda$ directly. Both panels are computed from the same sample path against the exact analytic benchmark, so any difference between them is attributable solely to the correction. The trace-corrected solution visibly tracks the benchmark more closely, in particular around the peak near $t \approx 0.55$, reducing the sup-norm error over $[0,1]$ from $3.60\times10^{-2}$ to $2.98\times10^{-2}$ and the $\mathcal L^2(0,1)$ error from $1.56\times10^{-2}$ to $1.25\times10^{-2}$.

    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/gbm_trace_correction_comparison.png}

Left: the trace-corrected ML-PIFLNN solution $\hat y_n$. Right: the uncorrected first-pass solution $y_n$, obtained from the same path by omitting $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma]$.     \label{fig:trace_correction_demo}

Substituting these approximations into the SVIE \eqref{eq:svie} transforms it into an operational matrix equation (OME):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ome}
    \mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = y_0\mathbf e^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) + \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf P_\alpha(t) \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) + \mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma](t),
\end{equation}

The vectors $\mathbf c, \mathbf c_b, \mathbf c_\sigma \in \mathbb R^{n+1}$ are initially unknown. We assemble a single parameter vector:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta}
    \boldsymbol \theta = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf c \\ \mathbf c_b \\ \mathbf c_\sigma \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{3(n+1)}.
\end{equation}

To discretize the system, we choose $N_c$ shifted Chebyshev training points, which we collect into the set $T_c \subseteq T$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chebyshev}
    t_i = \frac 12 \left ( 1 - \cos \frac{(2i - 1)\pi}{2N_c} \right ), \quad i \in \{1, \dots , N_c\},
\end{equation}
which effectively suppresses Runge-type boundary oscillations and optimizes our polynomial interpolation.

To set up the training procedure, we now impose the first assumption specific to the numerical method:
\begin{enumerate}[resume=assumptions,label=(A\arabic*)]
    \item \label{as:a4} $b(t,\cdot)$ and $\sigma(t,\cdot)$ are continuously differentiable in $y$, and there exists $L' > 0$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} \abs{\partial_yb(t,y)}+ \sup_{t\in T} \abs{\partial_y\sigma(t,y)} \leq L'
    \end{equation}
    uniformly in $y \in \mathbb R$.
\end{enumerate}
Assumption~\ref{as:a4} ensures that the residual $\mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta)$ to be defined below is continuously differentiable in the parameters $\boldsymbol \theta$, which is necessary for the gradient-based optimization that follows. Since a $C^1$ function with a uniformly bounded derivative is globally Lipschitz with the derivative bound as Lipschitz constant, \ref{as:a4} in fact implies \ref{as:a2} with $L \le L'$. We do not identify $L$ and $L'$, and carry both constants separately through \S\ref{sec:convergence}, since \ref{as:a2} alone (without \ref{as:a4}) is used for the well-posedness result of \S\ref{sec:well_posedness}.

We train the network parameter $\boldsymbol \theta = [\mathbf c^\top \; \mathbf c_b^\top \; \mathbf c_\sigma^\top]^\top$ by minimizing the loss function:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
    L(\mathbf c, \mathbf c_b, \mathbf c_\sigma) = L_{\mathrm{SDE}} + \lambda_bL_b + \lambda_\sigma L_\sigma, 
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_b$, $\lambda_\sigma > 0$ are regularizing weights, and the individual components are
\label{sec:collocation_loss}
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:loss_components}
    \begin{align}
        L_{\mathrm{SDE}} &= \frac 1{N_c}\sum_{i=1}^{N_c} \left ( \mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - y_0\mathbf e^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf P_\alpha(t_i) \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - \mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) \right )^2, \\
        L_b &= \frac 1{N_c} \sum_{i=1}^{N_c} \left ( \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - b(t_i, \mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i))\right )^2, \\
        L_\sigma &= \frac 1{N_c} \sum_{i=1}^{N_c} \left ( \mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - \sigma(t_i, \mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i))\right )^2,
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
$L_{\mathrm{SDE}}$ collocates the operational matrix equation \eqref{eq:ome} with the correction omitted, so minimizing \eqref{eq:loss} yields only a provisional parameter vector $\boldsymbol \theta^* = [\mathbf (c^*)^\top \; \mathbf (c_b^*)^\top \; \mathbf (c_\sigma^*)^\top]^\top$. The correction is a functional of the very diffusion coefficient we are solving for, so it cannot be assembled into the loss until $\boldsymbol \theta^*$ is known. We resolve this circularity with a two-pass scheme. The first, uncorrected pass minimizes \eqref{eq:loss} to obtain $\boldsymbol \theta^*$, from which $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*]$ is computed via the Malliavin derivative in Proposition \ref{prop:Dtheta}. The second, corrected pass re-solves the system with the OME block target adjusted, producing the final coefficients $\hat{\boldsymbol \theta}$ that determine the reported solution. The trace of the vector the scheme finally reports, $\tau_n[\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma]$, is not the trace that was subtracted. The difference does not vanish with $n$, and we account for it in \S\ref{sec:convergence}.

We now describe the two optimization techniques we utilize based on the class of problem. We first consider the case of affine SDEs. Let $b(t, y) = b_0(t) + b_1y$ and $\sigma(t, y) = \sigma_0(t) + \sigma_1y$. When the drift and diffusion are affine in $y$, the loss components \eqref{eq:loss_components} are naturally linear with respect to $\boldsymbol \theta$. Define the basis collocation matrix $\boldsymbol \Phi \in \mathbb R^{N_c \times (n+1)}$ with entries $\Phi_{ij} = M_j^\Lambda(t_i)$, and the corresponding operational collocation matrices
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol \Phi_P =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        (\mathbf P_\alpha(t_1)\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_1))^\top \\ \vdots \\ (\mathbf P_\alpha(t_{N_c})\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_{N_c}))^\top
    \end{bmatrix}, \quad
    \boldsymbol \Phi_S =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        (\mathbf S_\alpha\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_1))^\top \\ \vdots \\ (\mathbf S_\alpha\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_{N_c}))^\top
    \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{N_c \times (n+1)}.
\end{equation}
The loss function \eqref{eq:loss} simplifies to an exact linear least-squares problem:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\boldsymbol \theta} \norm{\mathbf K \boldsymbol \theta - \mathbf d}_2^2
\end{equation}
where the block system matrix $\mathbf K \in \mathbb R^{3N_c \times 3(n+1)}$ and target vector $\mathbf d \in \mathbb R^{3N_c}$ are assembled as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block_system}
    \mathbf K =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol \Phi & -\boldsymbol \Phi_P & -\boldsymbol \Phi_S \\
        -\sqrt{\lambda_b}b_1\boldsymbol \Phi & \sqrt{\lambda_b}\boldsymbol \Phi & \mathbf 0 \\
        -\sqrt{\lambda_\sigma}\sigma_1 \boldsymbol \Phi & \mathbf 0 & \sqrt{\lambda_\sigma}\boldsymbol \Phi
    \end{bmatrix}, \quad
    \mathbf d =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        y_0\mathbf 1_{N_c} \\ \sqrt{\lambda_b}\mathbf b_0 \\ \sqrt{\lambda_\sigma} \boldsymbol \sigma_0
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf 1_{N_c} = [1 \; \cdots \; 1]^\top \in \mathbb R^{N_c}$, $\mathbf b_0 = [b_0(t_1) \; \cdots \; b_0(t_{N_c})]^\top$, and $\boldsymbol \sigma_0 = [\sigma_0(t_1) \; \cdots \; \sigma_0(t_{N_c})]^\top$. Provided $\mathbf K \in \mathbb R^{3N_c\times3(n+1)}$ has full column rank, i.e.\ $3N_c \ge 3(n+1)$, equivalently $N_c\ge n+1$ (satisfied throughout \S\ref{sec:numerics}, where $N_c=64\ge n+1=41$ at the largest degree used), $\mathbf K^\top\mathbf K$ is invertible and the first, uncorrected pass computes the provisional parameter vector $\boldsymbol \theta^* = [(\mathbf c^*)^\top \; (\mathbf c_b^*)^\top \; (\mathbf c_\sigma^*)^\top]^\top$ using the Moore--Penrose inverse:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theta_star}
    \boldsymbol \theta^* = (\mathbf K^\top \mathbf K)^{-1}\mathbf K^\top \mathbf d = \mathbf K^+ \mathbf d.
\end{equation}
This is exactly the minimizer differentiated by Proposition \ref{prop:Dtheta} and Corollary \ref{cor:affine} to obtain $D_s\mathbf c_\sigma^*$, and hence the discrete Malliavin trace of Definition~\ref{def:trace} evaluated at the training points, $\boldsymbol \tau = [\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*](t_1) \; \cdots \; \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*](t_{N_c})]^\top$, via the quadrature of \S\ref{sec:malliavin_trace_derivation}. Since the correction enters the OME \eqref{eq:ome} only through the block collocated by $L_{\mathrm{SDE}}$, the second, corrected pass re-solves the same system \eqref{eq:block_system} against the trace-adjusted target
\begin{equation}\label{eq:d_corrected}
    \hat{\mathbf d} = \mathbf d - \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol \tau \\ \mathbf 0 \\ \mathbf 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
    \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = [(\hat{\mathbf c})^\top \; (\hat{\mathbf c}_b)^\top \; (\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma)^\top]^\top = \mathbf K^+ \hat{\mathbf d}.
\end{equation}

In both the affine and non-linear cases, we work with the stacked residual vector $\mathbf r = [\mathbf r_1^\top \; \mathbf r_2^\top \; \mathbf r_3^\top]^\top \in \mathbb R^{3N_c}$ with three blocks:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf r_1 &= \boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf c - \boldsymbol\Phi_P \mathbf c_b - \boldsymbol\Phi_S \mathbf c_\sigma - y_0\mathbf 1_{N_c}, \label{eq:r_ome} \\
    \mathbf r_2 &= \sqrt{\lambda_b} \left (\boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf c_b - b(\mathbf t, \boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf c) \right ), \label{eq:r_drift} \\
    \mathbf r_3 &= \sqrt{\lambda_\sigma} \left (\boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf c_\sigma - \sigma(\mathbf t, \boldsymbol\Phi \mathbf c) \right ), \label{eq:r_diffusion}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf t = [t_1 \; \cdots \; t_{N_c}]^\top$ are the collocation points. In the affine case, $b$ and $\sigma$ are linear, so $\mathbf r = \mathbf K \boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf d$ exactly. In the non-linear case, $\mathbf r_2$ and $\mathbf r_3$ become non-linear functions of $\boldsymbol \theta$.

In the case of a general non-linear drift and diffusion, the loss equation \eqref{eq:loss} is a non-linear least-squares problem
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\boldsymbol \theta} \frac 12 \norm{\mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta)}_2^2.
\end{equation}
We solve for $\boldsymbol \theta$ via damped Gauss--Newton updates:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gn_update}
    \boldsymbol \theta^{(k+1)} = \boldsymbol \theta^{(k)} - \xi_k (\nabla_{\boldsymbol \theta} \mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta^{(k)}))^+ \mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta^{(k)}),
\end{equation}
where $\nabla_{\boldsymbol \theta} \mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta) \in \mathbb R^{3N_c \times 3(n+1)}$ is the analytic Jacobian, and $\xi_k \in (0, 1]$ is a line-search dampening parameter. The iterations are initialized from the affine solution linearized around $y_0$ and, in the first, uncorrected pass, run to convergence at the provisional minimizer $\boldsymbol \theta^*$. As in the affine case, $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*]$ is computed from $\boldsymbol \theta^*$ via Proposition \ref{prop:Dtheta} and Definition \ref{def:trace} and then frozen at $\boldsymbol \tau = [\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*](t_1) \; \cdots \; \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*](t_{N_c})]^\top$. The OME residual block of $\mathbf r$ is replaced by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gn_corrected_residual}
    \hat{\mathbf r}_1(\boldsymbol \theta) = \mathbf r_1(\boldsymbol \theta) + \boldsymbol \tau,
\end{equation}
and the second, corrected pass repeats the Gauss--Newton iteration \eqref{eq:gn_update} on $\hat{\mathbf r} = [\hat{\mathbf r}_1^\top \; \mathbf r_2^\top \; \mathbf r_3^\top]^\top$, warm-started from $\boldsymbol \theta^*$, to convergence at the final, trace-corrected estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol \theta}$.

The final approximation is hence furnished to be the projection
\begin{equation}
    y(t) \approx \hat y_n(t) = \hat{\mathbf c}^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Operational matrices of fractional integration}
In this subsection, we explicitly construct the deterministic and stochastic operational matrices for integration, $\mathbf P_\alpha (t)$ and $\mathbf S_\alpha$ respectively.
\subsubsection{Deterministic operational matrix}
Let $\mathbf X_n(t) = [t^{\lambda_0} \; \dots \; t^{\lambda_n}]^\top$ denote the vector of M\"untz monomials. Applying the $\II$ operator to an arbitrary monomial $t^{\lambda_k}$ for $k \in \{0, \dots , n\}$ yields
\begin{equation}
    \II (t^{\lambda_k}) = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}s^{\lambda_k}\dd{s} = t^\alpha \frac{\Gamma(\lambda_k + 1)}{\Gamma(\lambda_k + \alpha + 1)}t^{\lambda_k}.
\end{equation}
Extending this across the entire vector $\mathbf X_n(t)$, we have
\begin{equation}
    \II \mathbf X_n(t) = t^{\alpha} \mathbf D_\alpha \mathbf X_n(t)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf D_\alpha = \diag \left ( \frac{\Gamma(\lambda_0 + 1)}{\Gamma(\lambda_0 + \alpha + 1)}, \frac{\Gamma(\lambda_1 + 1)}{\Gamma(\lambda_1 + \alpha + 1)}, \dots , \frac{\Gamma(\lambda_n + 1)}{\Gamma(\lambda_n + \alpha + 1)} \right ) \in \mathbb R^{(n+1) \times (n+1)}
\end{equation}

An arbitrary M\"untz--Legendre polynomial $M_k^\Lambda(t)$ is a linear combination of the M\"untz monomials $\{t^{\lambda_0}, \dots , t^{\lambda_k}\}$. As such, there exists an invertible lower-triangular matrix $\mathbf C \in \mathbb R^{(n+1) \times (n+1)}$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = \mathbf C \mathbf X_n(t) \iff \mathbf X_n(t) = \mathbf C^{-1}\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}
We may now evaluate the action of the $\II$ operator on the M\"untz--Legendre basis vector $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    \II \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = \II(\mathbf C\mathbf X_n(t)) = \mathbf C(\II \mathbf X_n(t)) = \mathbf C(t^\alpha\mathbf D_\alpha\mathbf X_n(t)) = t^\alpha\mathbf C \mathbf D_\alpha \mathbf C^{-1}\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}
As such, we may define the deterministic fractional operational matrix of integration as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf P_\alpha(t) = t^{\alpha}\mathbf C \mathbf D_\alpha \mathbf C^{-1} \in \mathbb R^{(n+1) \times (n+1)}
\end{equation}
Forming the matrix $\mathbf C^{-1}$ through standard matrix inversion is numerically unstable due to the exponential growth of the condition number of the matrix $\mathbf C$. We firstly assemble the matrix $\mathbf C$ row-by-row using the three term Jacobi recurrence formula. Post-multiplying $\mathbf C$ by $\mathbf D_\alpha$ yields the scaled matrix $\tilde{\mathbf C} = \mathbf C \mathbf D_\alpha$, where the $k$th column of $\mathbf C$ is scaled by the $k$th diagonal entry of $\mathbf D_\alpha$. The time-independent factor $\mathbf C \mathbf D_\alpha \mathbf C^{-1}$ satisfies the linear matrix equation $(\mathbf C \mathbf D_\alpha \mathbf C^{-1}) \mathbf C = \tilde{\mathbf C}$. Since the matrix $\mathbf C$ is lower-triangular, this factor is lower-triangular with non-zero diagonal entries, and each row may be resolved via exact triangular back-substitution.

\subsubsection{Stochastic operational matrix}
Consider the It\^o integral of an arbitrary M\"untz--Legendre polynomial $M_i^\Lambda(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    I_i(t) = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1} M_i^\Lambda(s)\dd{B_s}.
\end{equation}
We assemble an $(n+1)$-dimensional vector of these It\^o integrals: $\mathbf I_n(t) = [I_0(t) \; \cdots \; I_n(t)]^\top$. For a fixed realization of Brownian motion $B_t(\omega)$, each component of $\mathbf I_n(t)$ represents a continuous, non-differentiable sample path on $T$. We may approximate $\mathbf I_n(t)$ by its orthogonal projection onto $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:som}
    \mathbf I_n(t) \approx \mathbf S_\alpha\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf S_\alpha$ is the realization-dependent operational matrix. Expanding the $i$th component of~\eqref{eq:som}, we have
\begin{equation}
    I_i(t) = \sum_{j=0}^n (\mathbf S_\alpha)_{ij} M_j^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}
Taking the $\mathcal L^2(0, 1)$ inner product of both sides with respect to an arbitrary basis polynomial $\mathbf M_k^\Lambda(t)$ for $k \in \{0, \dots , n\}$:
\begin{equation}
    \ip{I_i, M_k^\Lambda}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} = \sum_{j=0}^n (\mathbf S_\alpha)_{ij} \ip{M_j^\Lambda, M_k^\Lambda}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)}.
\end{equation}
By the orthogonality of the M\"untz--Legendre polynomials~\eqref{eq:muntz_orthogonality}, we have
\begin{equation}
      (\mathbf S_\alpha)_{ik} = \frac{\ip{I_i, M_k^\Lambda}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)}}{\norm{M_k^\Lambda}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)}} = (2k\alpha+1)\int_0^1 I_i(s)M_k^\Lambda(s)\dd{s}.
\end{equation}
We may represent this compactly in matrix form. The stochastic fractional operational matrix of integration is given by
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf S_\alpha = \boldsymbol \Xi \boldsymbol \Omega^{-1},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol \Omega = \diag \left (1, \frac{1}{2\alpha + 1}, \dots, \frac{1}{2n\alpha + 1} \right )
\end{equation}
and $\boldsymbol \Xi \in \mathbb R^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}$ is the stochastic cross-covariance matrix whose elements are defined by the double integral
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xi}
    \Xi_{ij} = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^1 \left [ \int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}M_i^\Lambda(s)\dd{B_s} \right ] M_j^\Lambda(t) \dd{t}.
\end{equation}

Evaluating the double integral in~\eqref{eq:xi} requires integrating the It\^o integral $I_i(t)$ repeatedly for every sample path. We overcome this computational bottleneck by applying the stochastic Fubini theorem \cite{veraar2012stochastic} over the domain $0 \leq s \leq t \leq 1$. Interchanging the order of the outer deterministic integral and the inner It\^o integral yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xi_fubini}
    \Xi_{ij} = \int_0^1 \left [ \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_s^1 (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}M_j^\Lambda(t)\dd{t} \right ] M_i^\Lambda(s)\dd{B_s}
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
    K^{(\alpha)}_j = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_s^1 (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}M_j^\Lambda(t)\dd{t}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:xi_fubini} reduces to a single one-dimensional stochastic integral with a deterministic integrand:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xi_final}
    \Xi_{ij} = \int_0^1 K^{(\alpha)}_j(s)M_i^\Lambda(s)\dd{B_s}.
\end{equation}

Over a uniform fine temporal partition $\{0 = s_0 < s_1 < \cdots < s_{n^*} = 1\}$ with step size $\Delta s = 1/n^*$ (we take $n^* = 65{,}536$ throughout \S\ref{sec:numerics}) and Brownian increments $\Delta B_\ell = B_{s_\ell} - B_{s_{\ell-1}}$, the stochastic integral~\eqref{eq:xi_final} is approximated by the left-point (adapted) Riemann--Itô sum
\begin{equation}
    \Xi_{ij} \approx \sum_{\ell=1}^{n^*} K_j^{(\alpha)}(s_{\ell-1})M_i^\Lambda(s_{\ell-1})\Delta B_\ell,
\end{equation}
which evaluates the deterministic integrand at the left endpoint $s_{\ell-1}$ of each increment $[s_{\ell-1},s_\ell]$, consistent with the definition of the It\^o integral in \eqref{eq:xi_final}. Evaluating instead at the right endpoint $s_\ell$ would introduce an $O(1)$ It\^o--Stratonovich-type bias unrelated to the quadrature step $\Delta s$. The quadrature error of this sum, governed by $\Delta s$ and by $\|K_j^{(\alpha)}\|_{C^1}$, is not tracked as a separate term in Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}. We take $n^*$ large enough for it to be negligible relative to the five error sources that theorem does track, and do not analyze it further here. Since the coefficients of the Brownian increments are deterministic, they are computed once and stored in a static matrix $\mathbf T \in \mathbb R^{(n+1)^2 \times n^*}$. For a collection of $R$ sample paths with increment matrix $\Delta \mathbf B \in \mathbb R^{R \times n^*}$, the operational entries for all $R$ paths are computed simultaneously via the matrix product
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \mathbf B \mathbf T^\top \in \mathbb R^{R \times (n+1)^2}.
\end{equation}

 \subsection{M\"untz--Legendre functional link neural network}
% Include algorithm and graphic of architecture.
We now examine the architecture of the ML-PIFLNN. We adopt a single-layer functional link architecture that enhances the input space through a fractional feature map:
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \mathcal D_0 &= t \in T, \\
        \mathcal D_1 &= \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(\mathcal D_0), \\
        \mathcal D_2 &= y_n(t) = \mathbf c^\top \mathcal D_1.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\mathbf c \in \mathbb R^{n+1}$ is the primary trainable weight vector. By embedding non-integer powers directly into the network architecture, it possesses the capability to represent weakly singular fractional sample paths to machine accuracy with a minimal parameter count. To handle non-linear drift $b$ and diffusion $\sigma$ without introducing iterative numerical integration, the ML-PIFLNN utilizes two auxiliary linear projections parameterized by $\mathbf c_b, \mathbf c_\sigma \in \mathbb R^{(n+1)}$:
\begin{equation}
    b(t, y(t)) \approx b(t, y_n(t)) = \hat b(t) = \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t), \quad \sigma(t, y(t)) \approx \sigma(t, y_n(t)) = \hat \sigma(t) = \mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}

Unlike deep multilayer perceptrons where gradients with respect to weights involve chain-rule products of hidden Jacobian matrices, the gradient of the ML-PIFLNN output with respect to its weights is the basis vector itself:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\mathbf c} y_n(t) = \nabla_{\mathbf c_b} \hat b(t) = \nabla_{\mathbf c_\sigma} \hat \sigma(t) = \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}
As a consequence, all residuals in the OME~\eqref{eq:ome} depend linearly on the parameter vector $\boldsymbol \theta$~\eqref{eq:theta}.

\caption{Trace-corrected training of the ML-PIFLNN}
\label{alg:mlpiflnn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Fractional order $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$; M\"untz sequence $\Lambda = \{\lambda_k\}_{k=0}^n$; drift $b$, diffusion $\sigma$, initial condition $y_0$; collocation size $N_c$; regularizing weights $\lambda_b, \lambda_\sigma > 0$; Gauss--Newton tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GN}}$ and iteration cap $K_{\mathrm{GN}}$
\Ensure Trained, trace-corrected parameters $\hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = [\hat{\mathbf c}^\top \; \hat{\mathbf c}_b^\top \; \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top]^\top$

\Statex \textbf{Basis and operational matrix assembly}
\State Compute $\{\eta_k^{(m)}\}_{k=0}^m$ for $m = 0, \dots, n$ and form $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = [M_0^\Lambda(t) \; \cdots \; M_n^\Lambda(t)]^\top$ \Comment{\S\ref{sec:muntz_legendre}, $O(n^2)$}
\State Assemble the monomial change-of-basis matrix $\mathbf C$, its inverse $\mathbf C^{-1}$, and the diagonal matrix $\mathbf D_\alpha = \diag \left ( \Gamma(\lambda_k+1)/\Gamma(\lambda_k+\alpha+1) \right )$, then form $\mathbf P_\alpha(t) = t^\alpha \mathbf C \mathbf D_\alpha \mathbf C^{-1}$ \Comment{$O(n^3)$ via triangular back-substitution, once}
\State Sample the driving path $B$ on a fine reference grid, assemble the static tensor $\mathbf T$, the cross-covariance $\boldsymbol \Xi$, and $\boldsymbol \Omega$, then set $\mathbf S_\alpha = \boldsymbol \Xi \boldsymbol \Omega^{-1}$ \Comment{$O((n+1)^2n^*)$ time and memory, dominant cost (\S\ref{sec:cost})}

\Statex \textbf{Collocation}
\State Generate the Chebyshev nodes $T_c = \{t_i\}_{i=1}^{N_c}$ \Comment{$O(N_c)$}
\State Assemble $\boldsymbol \Phi$, $\boldsymbol \Phi_P$, $\boldsymbol \Phi_S$ at $T_c$ \Comment{$O(N_c(n+1))$}

\Statex \textbf{First pass: uncorrected solve}
\If {$b, \sigma$ affine in $y$}
    \State Assemble $\mathbf K, \mathbf d$ \eqref{eq:block_system} \Comment{$O(N_c(n+1)^2)$}
    \State $\boldsymbol \theta^* \gets \mathbf K^+ \mathbf d$ \Comment{Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse, $O(N_c(n+1)^2+(n+1)^3)$}
\Else
    \State Initialize $\boldsymbol \theta^{(0)}$ from the affine solution linearized about $y_0$
    \For{$k = 0, 1, \dots, K_{\mathrm{GN}} - 1$}
        \State $\boldsymbol \theta^{(k+1)} \gets \boldsymbol \theta^{(k)} - \xi_k \left ( \nabla_{\boldsymbol \theta} \mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta^{(k)}) \right )^+ \mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta^{(k)})$ \Comment{$O(N_c(n+1)^2+(n+1)^3)$ per iteration}
        \If{$\norm{\boldsymbol \theta^{(k+1)} - \boldsymbol \theta^{(k)}} < \varepsilon_{\mathrm{GN}}$}
            \State \textbf{break}
        \EndIf
    \EndFor

\State Extract $D_s \mathbf c_\sigma^* = \mathbf E_\sigma D_s \boldsymbol \theta^*$ \Comment{$O(n+1)$}
\State Evaluate the frozen trace $\boldsymbol \tau = [\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*](t_1) \; \cdots \; \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*](t_{N_c})]^\top$, $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*](t_i) = \int_0^{t_i} K_\alpha(t_i, s) (D_s \mathbf c_\sigma^*)^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s}$ \Comment{$O(N_c(n+1))$ given a fixed quadrature rule}

\Statex \textbf{Second pass: trace-corrected re-solve (once, not iterated to self-consistency)}
\If {$b, \sigma$ affine in $y$}

\EndIf

\State \Return $\hat{\boldsymbol \theta}$
\end{algorithmic}

\subsection{Computational cost}\label{sec:cost}
We collect here the costs annotated in Algorithm~\ref{alg:mlpiflnn}.

\textbf{Assembly.} The dominant cost is the static tensor $\mathbf T \in \mathbb R^{(n+1)^2 \times n^*}$ underlying the stochastic Fubini contraction of \S\ref{sec:proposed_method}, at $O((n+1)^2n^*)$ time and, more restrictively, memory: at the largest degree used in \S\ref{sec:numerics}, $n=32$ and $n^*=65{,}536$, storing $\mathbf T$ in double precision requires $(33)^2 \times 65{,}536 \times 8$ bytes $\approx 571$ MB. This is assembled once per $\alpha$ and reused across all $R$ sample paths and training iterations, but remains a real constraint at larger $n$ or finer $n^*$. Unimplemented mitigations include blocking the contraction over $n^*$ (accumulating $\boldsymbol\Xi$ incrementally in chunks) or using a coarser $n^*$ for $\mathbf T$ than for the reference solvers, provided the resulting quadrature error is verified negligible. The deterministic matrix $\mathbf P_\alpha(t)$ costs only $O(n^3)$ once, via the triangular back-substitution of \S\ref{sec:proposed_method}.

\textbf{Per-path solve.} The affine case is a single $O(N_c(n+1)^2+(n+1)^3)$ pseudoinverse solve. The non-linear case multiplies this by $K_{\mathrm{GN}}$ Gauss--Newton iterations. Crucially, $\mathbf S_\alpha$ is \emph{realization-dependent} (it is built from the actually-observed increments $\Delta B$ of each path), so this per-path cost is incurred independently for every one of the $R$ paths in a Monte Carlo batch. Only the assembly tensor $\mathbf T$ itself, not $\mathbf S_\alpha$, is shared across paths. The single trace-correction pass adds one further linear solve of the same $O(N_c(n+1)^2+(n+1)^3)$ order (or $K_{\mathrm{GN}}$ further Gauss--Newton iterations in the non-linear case), i.e.\ the two-pass scheme roughly doubles the per-path solve cost of a hypothetical uncorrected scheme.

\textbf{Contraction.} Evaluating $\Delta\mathbf B\mathbf T^\top$ for all $R$ paths simultaneously costs $O(Rn^*(n+1)^2)$, dominated by a single BLAS matrix product.

\textbf{Comparison.} A direct (non-fast) Euler--Maruyama scheme over $N$ time steps costs $O(N^2)$ per path due to the nonlocal memory kernel. The fast EM method of \cite{zhang2023fast} reduces this to $O(NN_{\exp})$ via a sum-of-exponentials approximation of the kernel. At matched accuracy (\S\ref{sec:numerics} shows both approaches converge at strong order $\alpha-1/2$, in $N$ for fEM and in $n$ for ML-PIFLNN), we have not run a wall-clock comparison between the two at equal error.

\section{Malliavin correction}\label{sec:malliavin}
The ML-PIFLNN fits its diffusion coefficient $\mathbf c_\sigma$ by solving a single least-squares problem enforced simultaneously across every collocation time $t_1,\dots,t_{N_c}\in T_c$. As a consequence, $\mathbf c_\sigma^*$ at the optimum is not a function of the Brownian path restricted to $[0,t]$ for any fixed $t$: it is influenced by the driving path at every collocation time, including times later than $t$. A coefficient with this property is said to be anticipating, or non-adapted, and it is exactly the property an It\^o integrand is required not to have. When the algebraic scheme forms the product $\mathbf c_\sigma^\top\mathbf S_\alpha\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)$, the result is consequently not automatically the It\^o integral of $\sigma(t,y_n(t))$ that the SVIE~\eqref{eq:svie} calls for. The gap between the two is a computable quantity that we identify in this section, and account for in our scheme. Malliavin derivatives and Malliavin-based (Skorokhod) integrals already play an established role elsewhere in stochastic numerics, most notably in Malliavin-weight methods for weak-error expansions and sensitivity (``Greeks'') computations in mathematical finance~\cite{fournieetal1999,ballytalay1996,kohatsuhiga2001}. Our use is different in kind: rather than bounding a weak-error expansion, we use the Malliavin derivative to compute, in closed form, the exact bias a global strong-approximation scheme incurs from fitting a non-adapted coefficient, and to remove it.

\subsection{Preliminaries}\label{sec:malliavin_prelim}
Let $H = \mathcal L^2(0,1)$, the space of deterministic square-integrable functions on $(0,1)$, with the standard inner product and norm:
\begin{equation}
    \ip{f, g}_H = \int_0^1 f(s)g(s)\dd{s}, \quad \norm{f}_H = \sqrt{\ip{f, f}_H}
\end{equation}
For $h\in H$, the Wiener integral
\begin{equation}
    B(h)=\int_0^1h(s)\dd{B_s}
\end{equation}
is a mean-zero Gaussian random variable with $\EE[B(h)^2]=\norm{h}_H^2$ (utilizing It\^o isometry), restricted to deterministic integrands.

Let $\mathcal S$ denote the class of smooth cylindrical random variables:
\begin{equation}
    f\left (B(h_1),\dots,B(h_k) \right ),
\end{equation}
where $k \in \mathbb N$, $h_1, \dots, h_k \in H$, and $f: \mathbb R^k \to \mathbb R$ is smooth with $f$ and all its partial derivatives being of at most polynomial growth.

\begin{definition}
    Let $F \in \mathcal S$. The \emph{Malliavin derivative} of $F$ is the $H$-valued random variables
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:malliavin_derivative_def}
        D_s F = \sum_{j=1}^k \pdv{x_j} f(B(h_1), \dots, B(h_k))h_j(s), \quad s \in (0, 1).
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

In particular, letting $k = 1$ and $f : x \mapsto x$, we have $D_sB(h) = h(s)$: the Malliavin derivative of a Wiener integral is simply its integrand. The operator $D$ also obeys the ordinary chain rule for $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ continuously differentiable with $f'$ being of polynomial growth:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:malliavin_chain_rule}
    D_s \phi(F) = \phi'(F)D_s F.
\end{equation}

We also introduce the Sobolev space for the Malliavin derivative $\mathbb D^{1, 2}$. For any $F \in \mathcal S$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mal_sob_norm}
    \norm{F}^2_{\mathbb D^{1,2}} = \EE (F^2) + \EE \left ( \int_0^1 (D_sF)^2\dd{s} \right ).
\end{equation}
The operator $D$ is closable from $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$ to $\mathcal L^2(\Omega; H)$ \cite{nualart2006malliavin,nualartnualart2018}: for a sequence $\{F_n\}$ with $F_n \in \mathcal S$ for all $n \in \mathbb N$, if $F_n \to 0$ in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$, the sequence $\{DF_n\}$ converges to $0$ in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega; H)$. This permits extending $D$ by continuity to the completion of $\mathcal S$ under the norm~\eqref{eq:mal_sob_norm}. A random variable $F\in\mathbb D^{1,2}$ if and only if it is square-integrable and its (extended) Malliavin derivative $DF$ is square-integrable as an $H$-valued random variable. We will proceed to show that $\boldsymbol \theta^* \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}$.

The Wiener integral $B(h)$ only accepts deterministic integrands. To accommodate the anticipating coefficient $\mathbf c_\sigma^*$, we need a stochastic integral that accepts a strictly larger class, at the cost of losing the requirement of adaptedness. Such an integral is obtained by defining it as the adjoint of $D$.
\begin{definition}\label{def:skorokhod}
    A process $u \in \mathcal L^2(\Omega \times(0,1))$ belongs to the domain of the \emph{divergence operator} $\delta$, written $u \in \Dom (\delta)$, if there exists a constant $c_u$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{\EE\ip{DF,u}_H}\le c_u\norm{F}_{\mathcal L^2(\Omega)}
    \end{equation}
    for all $F \in \mathbb D^{1,2}$. When this holds, $F \mapsto \EE (\ip{DF,u}_H)$ is a bounded linear functional on $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$, and by the Riesz representation theorem \cite{rudin1991functional} there is a unique $\delta(u)\in \mathcal L^2(\Omega)$ satisfying the duality relation
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:duality}
        \EE(F \delta(u))=\EE(\ip{DF,u}_H)
    \end{equation}
    for all $F \in \mathbb D^{1,2}$. The random variable $\delta(u)$ is the \emph{Skorokhod integral}, of $u$ \cite{skorokhod1975,nualart2006malliavin}.
\end{definition}
 
It is also written
\begin{equation}
    \int_0^1u_s\,\delta B_s
\end{equation}
to emphasize its role as a generalized stochastic integral. We use the operator notation $\delta(u)$ when the integrand is a process we have already named, and the displayed notation when it is not, reserving $\dd{B_s}$ for the It\^o integral so that the two are never confused.

The duality relation~\eqref{eq:duality} places no adaptedness requirement on $u$. It is precisely what allows $\delta$ to accept the anticipating quantities appearing later in this section. When $u$ happens to be adapted, however, $\delta$ reduces to the familiar integral.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ito_special_case}
    If $u \in \mathcal L^2(\Omega \times(0,1))$ is adapted and is square-integrable in time, then $u \in \Dom (\delta)$ and
    \begin{equation}
        \delta(u)=\int_0^1u_s\dd{B_s}.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The single algebraic fact that produces the trace correction is the following.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:multiplication_formula}
    Let $F\in\mathbb D^{1,2}$ and $u \in \Dom (\delta)$, with $Fu \in \Dom (\delta)$. Then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:multiplication_formula}
        F\delta(u) = \delta(Fu) + \ip{DF,u}_H.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Proofs for these propositions are presented in \cite{nualart2006malliavin,nualartnualart2018}.

Proposition~\ref{prop:ito_special_case} is also the prerequisite machinery for proving that once the anticipation of the fitted diffusion is removed in the limit as $n \to \infty$, the resulting Skorokhod integral of the (now adapted) integrand is simply the It\^o integral of the SVIE~\eqref{eq:svie}. We also note two consequences of Proposition~\ref{prop:multiplication_formula}. If $F$ is deterministic, $DF = 0$ and~\eqref{eq:multiplication_formula} reduces to $F\delta(u) = \delta(Fu)$: multiplying a Skorokhod integral by a deterministic scalar behaves exactly as expected, with no correction. If $F$ is random, the correction term $\ip{DF,u}_H$ is generally nonzero, and it vanishes only when $F$ happens to be Malliavin-differentiable in a direction orthogonal to $u$ in $H$.

We also report that this machinery is not specific to our scheme: extending stochastic calculus to accommodate anticipating integrands via the divergence operator, and developing the corresponding chain and product rules, is the subject of a substantial literature originating with \textcite{NualartPardoux1988}, to which we refer for the general theory underlying the constructions of this section.

\subsection{Derivation of the Malliavin correction}\label{sec:malliavin_trace_derivation}

We now derive the correction announced in the introduction to this section. Recall the construction of the stochastic operational matrix of integration: $\mathbf S_\alpha = \boldsymbol \Xi \boldsymbol \Omega^{-1}$, assembled with~\eqref{eq:xi_final}, along with the projection identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projection_identity}
    \mathbf S_\alpha\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = \mathbf I_n(t) - \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t), \quad I_j(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t,s)M_j^\Lambda(s)\dd{B_s},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_n(t)$ is the $\mathcal L^2(0,1)$ projection remainder of $\mathbf I_n$ onto the truncated basis. We let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:An}
    A_n(t) = (\mathbf c_\sigma^*)^\top\mathbf S_\alpha\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)
\end{equation}
for the stochastic term the algebraic scheme actually forms in the operational matrix equation~\eqref{eq:ome}. As argued in the introduction to this section, $\mathbf c_\sigma^*$ is not adapted, so Proposition~\ref{prop:ito_special_case} does not apply to $A_n(t)$, and there is no prior reason for it to equal any Itô integral. We now compute the gap.

Every entry of $\boldsymbol \Xi$ is, by construction, a Wiener integral of a deterministic integrand, so its Malliavin derivative follows directly from~\eqref{eq:malliavin_derivative_def}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:DS}
    Let $\mathbf k^{(\alpha)}(s) = [ K_0^{(\alpha)}(s) \; \cdots \; K_n^{(\alpha)}(s) ]^\top$. Then $\Xi_{ij} \in \mathbb D^{1,2}$ for every $i,j$, and for a.e.\ $s\in(0,1)$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:DXi}
        D_s \boldsymbol \Xi = \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \mathbf k^{(\alpha)}(s)^\top, \quad D_s\mathbf S_\alpha = \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\mathbf k^{(\alpha)}(s)^\top \boldsymbol \Omega^{-1}.
    \end{equation}
    In particular $D_s\mathbf S_\alpha$ is deterministic and of rank $1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    By~\eqref{eq:xi_final}, $\Xi_{ij}=B(h_{ij})$ with $h_{ij}(s)=K_j^{(\alpha)}(s)M_i^\Lambda(s)\in H$. This is a smooth cylindrical variable with $k=1$ and $f : x \mapsto x$, so $D_s\Xi_{ij}=h_{ij}(s)$, the $(i,j)$ entry of $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\mathbf k^{(\alpha)}(s)^\top$. The second identity follows since $\mathbf S_\alpha=\boldsymbol\Xi\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}$ with $\boldsymbol\Omega$ being deterministic.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:DPhiS}
    Let $g_i(s)=\ip{\mathbf k^{(\alpha)}(s),\boldsymbol\Omega^{-1}\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i)}$ for $i \in \{ 1,\dots,N_c \}$, and define the vector $\mathbf g(s)=[g_1(s)\; \cdots \;g_{N_c}(s)]^\top$. Then for a.e.\ $s\in(0,1)$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:DPhiS}
        D_s\boldsymbol\Phi_S=\mathbf g(s)\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)^\top.
    \end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    The $i$th row of $\boldsymbol \Phi_S$ is $(\mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i))^\top$ with $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i)$ being deterministic, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:DS}, $D_s(\mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i))=(D_s\mathbf S_\alpha)\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i)=\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\mathbf k^{(\alpha)}(s)^\top \boldsymbol \Omega^{-1} \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i)=g_i(s)\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)$.
\end{proof}

Unlike $\boldsymbol \Xi$, the fitted vector $\boldsymbol \theta^* =[(\mathbf c^*)^\top \; (\mathbf c_b^*)^\top \; (\mathbf c_\sigma^*)^\top]^\top$ is not itself a Wiener integral, and Lemma~\ref{lem:DS}'s mechanical computation does not apply to it. It is defined only implicitly, as the minimizer of the collocation loss defined in \S~\ref{sec:collocation_loss}, and its Malliavin derivative must be obtained by differentiating the optimality condition that characterizes it. To invoke the implicit function theorem \cite{rudin1976principles} on the first-order optimality condition, we require one further standing assumption.

\begin{enumerate}[resume=assumptions,label=(A\arabic*)]
    \item \label{as:a5} For every $n \in \mathbb N_0$ and a.e.\ Brownian realization, the Hessian $\mathbf H$ of~\eqref{eq:hessian_def} (note: $\mathbf H=\mathbf J^\top\mathbf J+\sum_kr_k\nabla^2r_k$ is the \emph{full} Hessian of $(1/2)\norm{\mathbf r}^2$, not the Gauss--Newton approximation $\mathbf J^\top\mathbf J$ that Algorithm~\ref{alg:mlpiflnn} actually uses in its update step \eqref{eq:gn_update}), arising from the training procedure of \S\ref{sec:collocation_loss}, is invertible at the uncorrected minimizer $\boldsymbol \theta^*$ of \eqref{eq:theta_star} (equivalently, the limit point of \eqref{eq:gn_update} in the non-linear case), and $\EE(\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}^p)$ is finite for some $p>2$.
\end{enumerate}

In the affine case, \ref{as:a5} together with full column rank of $\mathbf K$ is already enough: Corollary~\ref{cor:affine} below differentiates the explicit closed form $\boldsymbol\theta^*=(\mathbf K^\top\mathbf K)^{-1}\mathbf K^\top\mathbf d$ by ordinary, globally valid matrix calculus, with no implicit function theorem needed at all. The non-linear case is different in kind. There $\boldsymbol\theta^*$ is characterized only implicitly by $\mathbf F(\boldsymbol\theta^*,\mathbf S_\alpha) = \mathbf 0$, and the classical implicit function theorem gives a continuously differentiable local solution $\mathbf S_\alpha \mapsto \boldsymbol\theta^*(\mathbf S_\alpha)$ only on a neighborhood of each realized $\mathbf S_\alpha(\omega)$, of a radius that depends on $\|\mathbf H^{-1}(\omega)\|$ and is consequently itself random, a.s.\ finite but unbounded across $\omega$. The Malliavin chain rule, by contrast, requires composing $D_s$ with a function that is continuously differentiable with polynomially bounded derivative on the \emph{whole} of the relevant space, not merely on a random, shrinking neighborhood around one realization. A locally-defined implicit function does not by itself supply such an object, so appealing to the chain rule in the non-linear case is not justified by \ref{as:a5} alone. We therefore adopt the conclusion as a further standing assumption in this case, rather than as a theorem derived from \ref{as:a5}:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(A5b)]
    \item \label{as:a5b} In the non-linear case, the uncorrected minimizer $\boldsymbol\theta^*$, and likewise the corrected minimizer $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ of \S\ref{sec:proposed_method}, belong to $\mathbb D^{1,2}$, with Malliavin derivative given by formally differentiating the respective optimality condition through the implicit function theorem and the chain rule, i.e.\ by \eqref{eq:Dtheta} below (and its analogue at $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$, used in \S\ref{sec:convergence}).
\end{enumerate}

We adopt \ref{as:a5b} rather than proving it because a rigorous derivation requires machinery beyond \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a5}: either (i) a localization argument -- truncating on $\Omega_m = \{\|\mathbf H^{-1}\|\le m\}$ intersected with a uniform lower bound on the implicit function theorem's radius of validity there (which in turn requires a Lipschitz bound on $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathbf F$, i.e.\ a second-derivative bound on $b,\sigma$ beyond \ref{as:a4}), patching the resulting family of local charts into a single, globally $C^1$, bounded-derivative extension agreeing with $\boldsymbol\theta^*$ on $\Omega_m$, and passing to the limit $m\to\infty$ via the closedness of $D$ and the moment bound of \ref{as:a5}, in the spirit of the Picard-iterate argument used for $y(t)$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:mall_dev_y}, or (ii) an approximation argument through the Gauss--Newton iterates $\boldsymbol\theta^{(k)}$ of Algorithm~\ref{alg:mlpiflnn} themselves, each an elementary, globally defined function of $\mathbf S_\alpha$ with no implicit function theorem required at finite $k$, showing $\boldsymbol\theta^{(k)}\to\boldsymbol\theta^*$ in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$ together with a uniform-in-$k$ bound on $\EE\int_0^1\abs{D_s\boldsymbol\theta^{(k)}}^2\dd{s}$ obtained inductively from the Gauss--Newton recursion. We carry out neither argument in this revision, and record it as the most significant open item in \S\ref{sec:conclusion}.

We let $\mathbf J( \boldsymbol \theta,\mathbf S_\alpha ) = \partial_{\boldsymbol \theta} \mathbf r(\boldsymbol \theta, \mathbf S_\alpha)$ for the Jacobian of the collocation residual, and define the normal-equation map
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf F(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf S_\alpha)=\mathbf J(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf S_\alpha)^\top\mathbf r(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf S_\alpha),
\end{equation}
so that $\mathbf F(\boldsymbol\theta^*,\mathbf S_\alpha)=\mathbf 0$ identically in $\mathbf S_\alpha$, this being the first-order optimality condition for a minimizer of $\frac12\norm{\mathbf r}^2$. We write $D_s^{\exp}$ for the Malliavin derivative applied only to the explicit appearances of $\mathbf S_\alpha$ in a given expression, with $\boldsymbol \theta$ held fixed as a symbol. This is the Malliavin-calculus analogue of a partial derivative, as opposed to the total derivative, and is what separates the mechanical part of a computation (Lemma~\ref{lem:DS}, Corollary~\ref{cor:DPhiS}) from the part that requires solving for $D_s \boldsymbol \theta^*$ below.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:Dtheta}
    In the non-linear case, under Assumptions~\ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a5} and \ref{as:a5b}, $\boldsymbol \theta^* \in \mathbb D^{1,2}$ (Assumption~\ref{as:a5b}), and for a.e.\ $s \in (0,1)$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:hessian_def}
        \mathbf H = \mathbf J^\top \mathbf J + \sum_k r_k \nabla^2_{\boldsymbol \theta}r_k,
    \end{equation}
    evaluated at $\boldsymbol\theta^*$, is invertible, and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Dtheta}
        D_s \boldsymbol \theta^* = -\mathbf H^{-1} D_s^{\exp} \mathbf F(\boldsymbol \theta^*, \mathbf S_\alpha),
    \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation}
        D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F = (D_s^{\exp}\mathbf J)^\top\mathbf r+\mathbf J^\top D_s^{\exp}\mathbf r, \quad D_s^{\exp}\mathbf r = \begin{bmatrix}-(D_s\boldsymbol\Phi_S)\mathbf c_\sigma^*\\\mathbf 0\\\mathbf 0\end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}
    $D_s^{\exp}\mathbf J$ vanishing except in the block $\partial\mathbf r_1/\partial \mathbf c_\sigma = -\boldsymbol \Phi_S$, whose Malliavin derivative is $-D_s \boldsymbol \Phi_S$. With $\mathbf E_\sigma=[\mathbf 0 \; \mathbf 0 \; \mathbf I_{n+1}]$ the selection matrix onto the diffusion block,
    \begin{equation}
        D_s \mathbf c_\sigma^* = \mathbf E_\sigma D_s \boldsymbol \theta^*.
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    Fix a realization $\omega \in \Omega$. Then $\mathbf S_\alpha(\omega)$ is an ordinary matrix of real numbers, and the identity $\mathbf F (\boldsymbol\theta^*(\mathbf S_\alpha), \mathbf S_\alpha) = \mathbf 0$ holds for every such matrix in a neighborhood of $\mathbf S_\alpha(\omega)$, not merely at the single point $\mathbf S_\alpha(\omega)$ itself, since $\boldsymbol\theta^*$ is by definition the minimizer of $\frac 12 \norm{\mathbf r}^2$ for the corresponding realization of the collocation matrices. Under Assumption~\ref{as:a4}, $\mathbf r (\cdot, \mathbf S_\alpha(\omega))$ is continuously differentiable in $\boldsymbol\theta$, so $\mathbf F = \mathbf J^\top \mathbf r$ is continuously differentiable in $\boldsymbol\theta$ with $\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathbf F = \mathbf H$. Assumption~\ref{as:a5} gives that $\mathbf H$ is invertible at $\boldsymbol\theta^*$. The classical implicit function theorem therefore applies to $\mathbf F$ at $(\boldsymbol\theta^*, \mathbf S_\alpha(\omega))$: there is a neighborhood of $\mathbf S_\alpha(\omega)$ on which the equation $\mathbf F (\boldsymbol\theta, \mathbf S_\alpha) = \mathbf 0$ defines a unique continuously differentiable function $\mathbf S_\alpha \mapsto \boldsymbol\theta^*(\mathbf S_\alpha)$, agreeing with the actual least-squares minimizer, and satisfying
    \begin{equation}
        \pdv{\mathbf S_\alpha}\boldsymbol\theta^* \bigg|_{\mathbf S_\alpha(\omega)} = -\mathbf H^{-1} \pdv{\mathbf S_\alpha}\mathbf F \bigg|_{(\boldsymbol\theta^*, \mathbf S_\alpha(\omega))},
    \end{equation}
    exactly as in the finite-dimensional implicit function theorem, applied here entrywise across the matrix $\mathbf S_\alpha$. Composing this ordinary partial derivative with the Malliavin derivative $D_s \mathbf S_\alpha$ already computed in Lemma~\ref{lem:DS}, via the chain rule, gives, for a.e. $s \in (0, 1)$,
    \begin{equation}
        D_s \boldsymbol\theta^* = \pdv{\mathbf S_\alpha}{\boldsymbol\theta^*} \cdot D_s \mathbf S_\alpha = -\mathbf H^{-1} \left ( \pdv{\mathbf S_\alpha}{\mathbf F} \cdot D_s \mathbf S_\alpha \right ) = -\mathbf H^{-1} D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F,
    \end{equation}
    which is~\eqref{eq:Dtheta}. The last equality is simply the definition of $D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F$ as the chain-rule composition of the explicit $\mathbf S_\alpha$-dependence of $\mathbf F$ against $D_s \mathbf S_\alpha$. This computation is a valid, ordinary (real-variable) differentiation of the local implicit function furnished by the classical implicit function theorem at the fixed realization $\omega$. It identifies what $D_s\boldsymbol\theta^*(\omega)$ would have to equal \emph{if} $\boldsymbol\theta^*$ is Malliavin differentiable, but it does not by itself establish that $\boldsymbol\theta^*\in\mathbb D^{1,2}$, since $D_s$ is a closed operator defined by extension from smooth cylindrical random variables (\S\ref{sec:malliavin_prelim}), not a pathwise notion, and the implicit function furnished above is defined only on an $\omega$-dependent neighborhood rather than globally, which is exactly what Assumption~\ref{as:a5b} supplies in place of a proof.

    As a consistency check on Assumption~\ref{as:a5b}, we record that the formula it postulates is at least automatically square-integrable under \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a5} alone, so the assumption does not smuggle in a hypothesis that conflicts with the standing ones. By~\eqref{eq:Dtheta} and the definition of the operator norm, $\abs{D_s \boldsymbol\theta^*} \le \norm{\mathbf H^{-1}} \abs{D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F}$ for a.e. $s$, so that
    \begin{equation}
        \int_0^1 \abs{D_s \boldsymbol\theta^*}^2 \dd{s} \le \norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}^2 \int_0^1 \abs{D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F}^2 \dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    Taking expectations and applying H\"older's inequality \cite{folland1999real} with exponents $p/2$ and $p/(p-2)$ (recall $p > 2$ from Assumption~\ref{as:a5}),
    \begin{equation}
        \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \abs{D_s \boldsymbol\theta^*}^2 \dd{s} \right ) \leq \left ( \EE (\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}^p) \right )^{2/p} \left ( \EE \left ( \left ( \int_0^1 \abs{D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F}^2 \dd{s} \right )^{p/(p-2)} \right ) \right )^{(p-2)/p}.
    \end{equation}
    The first factor is finite by the moment bound of Assumption~\ref{as:a5}. The second is finite under Assumptions~\ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a3}, since $D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F$ is, as we show below, built from the bounded deterministic kernel $D_s \boldsymbol\Phi_S$ together with $\mathbf c_\sigma^*$ and $\mathbf r$, both of which inherit finite moments of every order from the growth conditions already imposed on the SVIE solution and the collocation residual. Together with $\EE (\boldsymbol\theta^{*2}) < \infty$, also from Assumptions~\ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a3}, this confirms the second-moment finiteness that membership in $\mathbb D^{1,2}$ requires, without on its own establishing the weak-derivative membership that Assumption~\ref{as:a5b} instead postulates.
 
    It remains to compute $D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F$ explicitly. Differentiating $\mathbf F = \mathbf J^\top \mathbf r$ by the product rule, holding $\boldsymbol\theta$ fixed as a symbol,
    \begin{equation}
        D_s^{\exp}\mathbf F = (D_s^{\exp}\mathbf J)^\top \mathbf r + \mathbf J^\top D_s^{\exp}\mathbf r.
    \end{equation}
    Of the three residual blocks stacked in $\mathbf r = [\mathbf r_1^\top \; \mathbf r_2^\top \; \mathbf r_3^\top]^\top$ (the SDE, drift-projection, and diffusion-projection residuals of \S~\ref{sec:collocation_loss}), only the SDE block $\mathbf r_1$ depends explicitly on $\mathbf S_\alpha$, through the term $-\boldsymbol\Phi_S \mathbf c_\sigma$ appearing in its definition. The drift and diffusion-projection blocks $\mathbf r_2, \mathbf r_3$ involve $\mathbf S_\alpha$ only implicitly, through $\boldsymbol\theta$ itself, and so contribute nothing to $D_s^{\exp}$. Hence $D_s^{\exp}\mathbf r_2 = D_s^{\exp}\mathbf r_3 = \mathbf 0$, and, by the product rule together with Corollary~\ref{cor:DPhiS},
    \begin{equation}
        D_s^{\exp}\mathbf r_1 = -(D_s \boldsymbol\Phi_S)\mathbf c_\sigma^*,
    \end{equation}
    giving the stated block form of $D_s^{\exp}\mathbf r$. The same reasoning applied to $\mathbf J = \partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}\mathbf r$ shows $D_s^{\exp}\mathbf J$ vanishes in every entry except the block $\partial \mathbf r_1 / \partial \mathbf c_\sigma = -\boldsymbol\Phi_S$, whose Malliavin derivative is $-D_s \boldsymbol\Phi_S$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:DPhiS}.
 
    Finally, since $\boldsymbol\theta^* = [(\mathbf c^*)^\top \; (\mathbf c_b^*)^\top \; (\mathbf c_\sigma^*)^\top]^\top$ and $\mathbf E_\sigma = [\mathbf 0 \; \mathbf 0 \; \mathbf I_{n+1}]$ selects the last $n+1$ coordinates of any vector conformal with $\boldsymbol\theta^*$, applying $\mathbf E_\sigma$ to~\eqref{eq:Dtheta} gives $D_s \mathbf c_\sigma^* = \mathbf E_\sigma D_s \boldsymbol\theta^*$ directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:affine}
    Suppose $b(t,y)=b_0(t)+b_1y$ and $\sigma(t,y)=\sigma_0(t)+\sigma_1y$, so that $\mathbf r=\mathbf K\boldsymbol\theta-\mathbf d$ with $\mathbf K,\mathbf d$ as in~\eqref{eq:block_system}, and suppose $\mathbf K$ has full column rank. Then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Dtheta_affine}
        D_s\boldsymbol\theta^* = -\mathbf K^+(D_s\mathbf K)\boldsymbol\theta^*-(\mathbf K^\top\mathbf K)^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf r^*,
        \qquad
        \mathbf r^*=\mathbf K\boldsymbol\theta^*-\mathbf d,
    \end{equation}
    where $D_s\mathbf K$ vanishes except in the block corresponding to $\boldsymbol\Phi_S$, equal to $-D_s\boldsymbol\Phi_S=-\mathbf g(s)\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)^\top$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    Full column rank of $\mathbf K$ (satisfied throughout \S\ref{sec:numerics}, where $N_c=64 \ge n+1 = 41$ at the largest degree used) makes $\mathbf A := \mathbf K^\top\mathbf K$ invertible, so $\boldsymbol\theta^* = \mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf K^\top\mathbf d$ with $\mathbf d$ independent of $s$ (it depends only on $y_0$, $b_0$, $\sigma_0$, none of which involve the anticipating coefficients). Applying $D_s$ termwise,
    \begin{equation}
        D_s\boldsymbol\theta^* = -\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf A)\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf K^\top\mathbf d + \mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf d = -\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf A)\boldsymbol\theta^* + \mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf d,
    \end{equation}
    using $D_s(\mathbf A^{-1}) = -\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf A)\mathbf A^{-1}$. Since $D_s\mathbf A = (D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf K + \mathbf K^\top(D_s\mathbf K)$,
    \begin{equation}
        -\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf A)\boldsymbol\theta^* = -\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf K\boldsymbol\theta^* - \mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf K^\top(D_s\mathbf K)\boldsymbol\theta^* = -\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf K\boldsymbol\theta^* - \mathbf K^+(D_s\mathbf K)\boldsymbol\theta^*,
    \end{equation}
    using $\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf K^\top = \mathbf K^+$. Adding the remaining term and combining, $-\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf K\boldsymbol\theta^* + \mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf d = \mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top(\mathbf d - \mathbf K\boldsymbol\theta^*) = -\mathbf A^{-1}(D_s\mathbf K)^\top\mathbf r^*$, which is exactly the stated formula.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:affine} therefore establishes $\boldsymbol\theta^*\in\mathbb D^{1,2}$ \emph{unconditionally} in the affine case, without invoking Assumption~\ref{as:a5b}: the differentiation above uses only ordinary matrix calculus applied to an explicit closed form that is valid on the whole of the relevant space, not a locally-defined implicit function, so none of the difficulty discussed prior to Assumption~\ref{as:a5b} arises. Assumption~\ref{as:a5b} is needed only for the non-linear (Gauss--Newton) case, i.e.\ for Example~\ref{ex:sqrt} and Example~\ref{ex:trig} in \S\ref{sec:numerics}. Example~\ref{ex:ou}(ii) and Example~\ref{ex:gbm} are affine and are consequently covered by Corollary~\ref{cor:affine} alone.

We are now ready to state the exact formulation of the trace.

\begin{definition}\label{def:trace}
    Let $\mathbf c\in\mathbb D^{1,2}(\mathbb R^{n+1})$. For $t\in T$, the \emph{Malliavin trace} of the M\"untz--Legendre collocation scheme at $\mathbf c$ is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:trace}
        \tau_n[\mathbf c](t) = \int_0^tK_\alpha(t,s)(D_s\mathbf c)^\top\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The trace is what stands between the algebraic stochastic term $A_n(t)$ of~\eqref{eq:An} and a true Skorokhod integral of the finite-dimensional diffusion approximation: subtracting $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^*]$ from $A_n$ leaves such an integral, up to the projection remainder $\boldsymbol\varepsilon_n(t)$, which is a separate source of error unrelated to adaptedness. We defer the precise statement and its proof to Lemma~\ref{lem:trace_identity} in \S\ref{sec:convergence}, where the identity is needed at the corrected coefficient $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ rather than at $\mathbf c_\sigma^*$, and where proving it once at an arbitrary $\mathbf c$ serves both.

\section{Convergence analysis}\label{sec:convergence}
In this section we prove a mean-square error bound for the corrected ML-PIFLNN estimator $\hat y_n$ produced by the two-pass scheme of \S\ref{sec:proposed_method}. The argument proceeds in three steps. First, \S5.1 below fixes notation for the corrected coefficients and states two additional regularity hypotheses, \ref{as:a6} and \ref{as:a7}, that bound the coefficients and the fitted drift and diffusion uniformly and control the Malliavin derivative of the fitted diffusion. Second, Proposition~\ref{prop:error_rep} decomposes the global error $\hat y_n(t) - y(t)$ into four explicit terms: two ordinary drift terms controlled by the training loss and the Lipschitz hypothesis \ref{as:a2}, a stochastic term controlled by the Skorokhod isometry of Lemma~\ref{lem:sko_iso} and the Malliavin consistency defect of \ref{as:a7}, and a residual block controlled by the training loss together with the frozen-trace defect of Proposition~\ref{prop:trace_defect_bound} and the projection remainder of the stochastic operational matrix. Third, Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence} bounds and sums the four terms. The two terms carrying the unknown error itself assemble into a weakly singular Volterra inequality, which the generalized Gronwall inequality of Lemma~\ref{lem:conv_gronwall} closes into the explicit five-term rate of \eqref{eq:five_term_rate}, exhibiting the optimizer error, the collocation error, the stochastic projection error, the Malliavin consistency defect, and the frozen-trace defect as the five independent sources of error in the scheme.

\subsection{Preliminaries}
We fix for this whole section, the truncation degree $n$, the number of collocation points $N_c$, and the two-pass output $\hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = [\hat{\mathbf c}^\top \; \hat{\mathbf c}_b^\top \; \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top]^\top$, and define
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_n(t) = \hat{\mathbf c}^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t), \quad \hat b (t) = \hat{\mathbf c}_b^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t), \quad \hat \sigma (t) = \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t).
\end{equation}
We define the deviations of the auxiliary projections to be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:xi_deviations}
    \xi_b(t) = \hat b(t) - b(t, \hat y_n(t)), \quad \xi_\sigma(t) = \hat \sigma(t) - \sigma(t, \hat y_n(t)),
\end{equation}
and for the projection remainder of the stochastic operational matrix of integration we define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deltaS}
    \delta_S(n)^2 = \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2).
\end{equation}

To the standing hypotheses \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a5b}, we add two further assumptions.
\begin{enumerate}[resume=assumptions,label=(A\arabic*)]
    \item \label{as:a6} There exist positive constants $\Theta$ and $\Theta'$, and an exponent $q \in (0, 1]$ independent of the truncation degree $n$ and collocation count $N_c$ such that the vectors $\abs{\hat{\mathbf c}}, \abs{\hat{\mathbf c}_b}, \abs{\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma}, \abs{\mathbf c^*_\sigma} \leq \Theta$ a.s.\ and the maps $t \mapsto \hat y_n(t)$, $t \mapsto \hat b(t)$, $t \mapsto \hat \sigma(t)$, $t \mapsto b(t, \hat y_n(t))$, $t \mapsto \sigma(t, \hat y_n(t))$, $t \mapsto \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t)$, and $t \mapsto R(t)$ of \eqref{eq:corrected_residual} are H\"older continuous of order $q$ on $T$ with H\"older seminorm bounded by $\Theta'$ a.s.
    \item \label{as:a7} Let $\mathbb L^{1,2} = \mathcal L^2((0, 1); \mathbb D^{1, 2})$. For every $n \in \mathbb N_0$, $\hat \sigma \in \mathbb L^{1, 2}$, and
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
        \item there exists a uniform bound $\Theta_D > 0$ independent of $n$ such that
        \begin{equation}
            \sup_{n \in \mathbb N_0} \sup_{t \in T} \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2\abs{D_r\hat \sigma(s)}^2 \dd{s}\dd{r}\right ) \leq \Theta_D^2
        \end{equation}
        and,
        \item the Malliavin consistency defect, so named because it measures the failure of the fitted diffusion to agree with the exact one in Malliavin derivative rather than merely in value,
        \begin{equation}
            \varepsilon_D(n)^2 = \sup_{t \in T} \int_0^1 \int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \EE \left ( \abs{D_r\hat \sigma(s) - \partial_y\sigma (s, y(s))D_ry(s)}^2 \right )\dd{s}\dd{r}
        \end{equation}
        satisfies $\varepsilon_D(n) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

We now proceed to state the preliminary lemmata we invoke in the proof of convergence.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:P_exact}
    For every $t \in T$ and every $\mathbf c_b \in \mathbb R^{n+1}$,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf P_\alpha(t) \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s) \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\dd{s}
    \end{equation}
    with no truncation error.
\end{lemma}
The proof for this lemma follows from the construction of the operational matrix $\mathbf P_\alpha(t)$ in \S\ref{sec:proposed_method}.

We next state a lemma governing the decay of the projection error for the stochastic operational matrix. However, we first require a preliminary result tying the projection error of a function onto the M\"untz--Legendre basis and its H\"older continuity.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:jackson}
    Let $\Lambda = \{n\alpha\}$ for $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$ be a M\"untz sequence, and $\{M_n^\Lambda\}$ be the corresponding M\"untz--Legendre orthogonal (not orthonormal, see \eqref{eq:muntz_orthogonality}) basis in $\mathcal L^2(0, 1)$. Let $P_n$ denote the $\mathcal L^2(0, 1)$ projection onto the space $V_n = \linspan \{M_0^\Lambda, \dots , M_{n-1}^\Lambda\}$.

    If $f$ is H\"older-$\beta$ continuous on $[0, 1]$, then
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{f - P_nf}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} = O(n^{-\beta}).
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We invoke the Müntz-Jackson theorem \cite{GOLITSCHEK1991337}: if $f$ is Hölder-$\beta$ continuous on $[0, 1]$, then there exists a Müntz polynomial $q_{n-1}$ in the truncated span $V_n = \linspan\{M_0^\Lambda, \dots, M_{n-1}^\Lambda\}$ such that
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:jackson_muntz}
        \norm{f - q_{n-1}}_{\infty} \le C_\beta n^{-\beta}.
    \end{equation}
    We also invoke the projection theorem \cite{rudin1991functional}: let $V_n$ be a closed subspace of $\mathcal L^2(0, 1)$. For any $f \in \mathcal L^2(0, 1)$, the orthogonal projection $P_n f$ satisfies
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:projection}
        \norm{f - P_nf}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} = \inf_{v \in V_n} \norm{f - v}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)}.
    \end{equation}
    
    For any function $g$ on $[0, 1]$, the elementary inequality $\norm{g}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} \le \norm{g}_{\infty}$ holds. By \eqref{eq:jackson_muntz}, therefore,
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{f - q_{n-1}}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} \le \norm{f - q_{n-1}}_{\infty} \le C_\beta n^{-\beta}.
    \end{equation}
    
    Since $q_{n-1} \in V_n$ and the projection $P_n f$ minimizes the distance to $f$ over $V_n$ by equation \eqref{eq:projection}, we have
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{f - P_nf}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} \le \norm{f - q_{n-1}}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} 
        \le C_\beta n^{-\beta}.
    \end{equation}
    
    The bound $\norm{f - P_nf}_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)} = O(n^{-\beta})$ follows immediately.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:proj_remainder}
    Let $\boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t) = \mathbf I_n(t) - \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)$, and let $\delta_S(n)$ be as defined in \eqref{eq:deltaS}. For every $\mathbf c \in \mathbb R^{n+1}$ with $\abs{\mathbf c} \leq \Theta$ a.s. it follows that,
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} \EE \left ( \abs{\mathbf c^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2 \right ) \leq \abs{\Theta \delta_S(n)}^2,
    \end{equation}
    and $\delta_S(n) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{remark}
    We deliberately state only the qualitative decay $\delta_S(n) \to 0$ here, and not an explicit polynomial rate. A naive attempt to obtain one by summing the $n+1$ per-component Jackson bounds of Lemma~\ref{lem:jackson}, each $O(n^{-\beta})$, produces $\sup_{t\in T}\EE(\abs{\boldsymbol\varepsilon_n(t)}^2) = O(n^{1-2\beta})$, which \emph{diverges} for every admissible $\beta<\alpha-1/2$ since $\alpha\in(1/2,1)$ forces $1-2\beta>0$. Extracting a genuine rate requires a joint (rather than component-wise) approximation-theoretic argument that exploits the shared regularity of the bivariate kernel $K_\alpha$ across all $n+1$ components simultaneously, together with a uniform bound on the a.s.-random H\"older constant furnished by Kolmogorov's continuity theorem \cite{DaPratoZabczyk2014}. We confine the theory below to the qualitative statement, which suffices for Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
    To derive the bound, we simply utilize the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for the inner product:
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{\mathbf c^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2 \leq \abs{\mathbf c^\top}^2 \abs{\boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2 \leq \Theta^2 \abs{\boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2.
    \end{equation}
    We now proceed to take the expectation followed by the supremum over $t \in T$:
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\mathbf c^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2) \leq \Theta^2 \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2).
    \end{equation}
    From \eqref{eq:deltaS}, we know that the right-hand supremum is precisely $\abs{\delta_S(n)}^2$, establishing the bound
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\mathbf c^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)}^2) \leq \abs{\Theta \delta_S(n)}^2.
    \end{equation}

    It remains to show $\delta_S(n) \to 0$. Each component of $\mathbf I_n(t)$,
    \begin{equation}
        I_j(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t,s) M_j^\Lambda(s)\dd{B_s}, \qquad j = 0, \dots, n,
    \end{equation}
    is a stochastic convolution against the kernel $K_\alpha(t,s) = (t-s)^{\alpha-1}/\Gamma(\alpha)$, square-integrable for $\alpha>1/2$ by Remark~\ref{rem:kernel_l2}. Kolmogorov's continuity theorem \cite{DaPratoZabczyk2014} therefore gives, for a.e.\ $\omega$, a modification of $I_j(\cdot,\omega)$ that is H\"older continuous of every order $\beta<\alpha-1/2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:jackson} applied pathwise, $\|I_j(\cdot,\omega) - P_nI_j(\cdot,\omega)\|_{\mathcal L^2(0,1)} \to 0$ as $n\to\infty$, for each fixed $j$, almost surely. Since $\mathbf S_\alpha\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)$ is, by construction \eqref{eq:som}, exactly the $\mathcal L^2(0,1)$-orthogonal projection of $\mathbf I_n$ onto $\linspan\{M_0^\Lambda,\dots,M_n^\Lambda\}$ evaluated at $t$, and $n$ is finite at every stage, $\varepsilon_j(t,\omega) \to 0$ for a.e.\ $\omega$ and every $t$ as $n \to \infty$, for each fixed $j$. Letting $n\to\infty$ with $j$ fixed and invoking completeness of the M\"untz--Legendre system (Lemma~\ref{lem:muntz_szasz_completeness}) together with dominated convergence \cite{folland1999real} under the moment bound of Assumption~\ref{as:a6} gives $\EE(\abs{\varepsilon_j(t)}^2) \to 0$ for every $t\in T$ and every fixed $j$. A diagonal argument over the finitely many components $j=0,\dots,n$ present at truncation level $n$ then yields $\sup_{t\in T}\EE(\abs{\boldsymbol\varepsilon_n(t)}^2) \to 0$, i.e.\ $\delta_S(n)\to 0$, as claimed.
\end{proof}

The generalized Gronwall inequality, and with it the constants of the convergence theorem below, are stated in terms of the Mittag-Leffler function, which plays for fractional equations the role the exponential plays for classical ones. We record its definition here.
\begin{definition}\label{def:mittag_leffler}
    For $\alpha, \beta > 0$, the two-parameter \emph{Mittag-Leffler function} is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:mittag_leffler}
        E_{\alpha, \beta}(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{z^k}{\Gamma(\alpha k + \beta)}, \quad z \in \mathbb C,
    \end{equation}
    and we abbreviate $E_\alpha(z) = E_{\alpha, 1}(z)$. The series is entire in $z$ for every $\alpha, \beta > 0$. On the real half-line, $E_\alpha$ is positive and strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$, and at $\alpha = 1$ it degenerates to the exponential, $E_1(z) = E_{1,1}(z) = e^z$.
\end{definition}

We now invoke the generalized Gronwall inequality from \cite{YE20071075}.
\begin{lemma}[Generalized Gronwall inequality \cite{YE20071075}]\label{lem:conv_gronwall}
    Let $\beta \in (0, 1]$, $C_G > 0$, $a : T \mapsto [0, \infty)$ be locally integrable, and $h : T \to [0, \infty)$ be locally integrable and satisfy
    \begin{equation}
        h(t) \leq a(t) + C_G\int_0^t (t - s)^{\beta - 1}h(s)\dd{s},
    \end{equation}
    for $t \in T$. Then,
    \begin{equation}
        h(t) \leq a(t) + \int_0^t a(s)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(C_G\Gamma(\beta))^k}{\Gamma(k\beta)}(t - s)^{k\beta-1}\dd{s},
    \end{equation}
    for $t \in T$, the series converging uniformly on compact subsets of $[0, 1]$. If in addition, the function $a$ is non-decreasing, then $h(t) \leq a(t)E_\beta(C_G\Gamma(\beta)t^\beta)$.
\end{lemma}

We also state the anticipating equivalent to It\^o isometry from \cite{nualart2006malliavin}, which we term Skorokhod isometry.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:sko_iso}
    Let $v \in \mathbb D^{1,2}(\mathcal L^2(0, 1))$. Then, $v \in \Dom(\delta)$ and
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:sko_iso}
        \EE (\abs{\delta(v)}^2) = \EE (\norm{v}^2_{\mathcal L^2(0, 1)}) + \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \int_0^1 D_rv_sD_sv_r\dd{s}\dd{r}\right ).
    \end{equation}
    In particular,
    \begin{equation}
        \EE (\abs{\delta(v)}^2) \leq \EE \left ( \int_0^1 v_s^2 \dd{s} \right ) + \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \int_0^1 (D_rv_s)^2 \dd{s}\dd{r} \right ).
    \end{equation}
    If $v$ is adapted, the second integral on the right in \eqref{eq:sko_iso} vanishes, and the equation is the classical It\^o isometry.
\end{lemma}

We now derive the Malliavin derivative of our exact solution along with a bound of its squared expectation.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mall_dev_y}
    Under \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a4}, the solution $y$ to the SVIE \eqref{eq:svie} satisfies $y(t) \in \mathbb D^{1,2}$ for every $t \in T$, with $D_ry(t) = 0$ for $r > t$, and for a.e.\ $r < t$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:mall_dev_y}
    \begin{aligned}
        D_ry(t) &= K_\alpha(t, r)\sigma(r, y(r)) \\
        &+ \int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s} \\
        &+ \int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{B_s}.
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    Furthermore, define the uniform bound
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Csigma}
        C_\sigma^2 = \sup_{s \in T} \EE (\abs{\sigma(s, y(s))}^2).
    \end{equation}
    There exists a constant $C_D > 0$ depending on the fractional order $\alpha$, the Lipschitz constant $L$ from \ref{as:a2}, and the uniform bound $C_\sigma$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \EE (\abs{D_ry(t)}^2) \leq C_D(t - r)^{2\alpha - 2}
    \end{equation}
    for $0 \leq r < t \leq 1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We first prove that $y \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}$, i.e. it is Malliavin differentiable. Since $y_0$ is $\mathcal F_0$-measurable, $D_r y_0 = 0$. The drift integral is not a Wiener integral, and is in $\mathbb D^{1, 2}$ provided the drift function $b(t, y(t))$ is. Since the diffusion It\^o integral is adapted, it is also in $\mathbb D^{1, 2}$ provided the diffusion function $\sigma(t, y(t))$ is (boundedness is not required for this membership). Note that both $b$ and $\sigma$ depend explicitly on the solution $y(t)$. As such, we resolve this via Picard iteration and the Malliavin chain rule on Lipschitz functions.

    Define the iterative sequence
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:picard_iter}
        y^{(0)}(t) = y_0, \quad y^{(k+1)}(t) = y_0 + \int_0^t K_{\alpha}(t, s)b(s, y^{(k)}(s))\dd{s} + \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\sigma(s, y^{(k)}(s))\dd{B_s}.
    \end{equation}
    We induct on $k \in \mathbb N_0$. Since the base case $y_0$ is $\mathcal F_0$-measurable, it does not depend on Brownian perturbations and as such, $D_ry_0 = 0$ for all $r$. We now assume that $y^{(k)} \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}$. By \ref{as:a2}, the drift $b(t, \cdot)$ and diffusion $\sigma(t, \cdot)$ are Lipschitz. We utilize the chain rule for the Malliavin derivative for Lipschitz functions \cite{nualart2006malliavin}. If $f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is Lipschitz and $X \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}$, then $f(X) \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}$ and
    \begin{equation}
        D_r f(X) = f'(X)D_rX.
    \end{equation}
    provided the derivative $f'$ exists a.e. We apply this theorem to $b(t, y^{(k)}(t))$ and $\sigma(t, y^{(k)}(t))$. Since $y^{(k)} \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}$, by the inductive hypothesis, and $b(t, \cdot)$ and $\sigma(t, \cdot)$ being Lipschitz, we have
    \begin{equation}
        b(s, y^{(k)}(s)), \sigma(s, y^{(k)}(s)) \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}.
    \end{equation}
    Both the integrals in \eqref{eq:picard_iter} now involve Malliavin differentiable integrands, ensuring they are both elements of $\mathbb D^{1, 2}$, and therefore,
    $y^{(k+1)}(s) \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}.$
    By the Picard iteration method \cite{MAO1995281}, the iterative sequence $\{y^{(n)}\}$ converges to $y$ in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega; \mathcal C(T))$. In particular $y^{(k)}(t) \to y(t)$ in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$ for every fixed $t \in T$. Since $D$ is a \emph{closed} operator from $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$ to $\mathcal L^2(\Omega\times(0,1))$ (it is \emph{not} that $\mathbb D^{1,2}$ is a closed subspace of $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$, which is false: $\mathbb D^{1,2}$ is a dense, proper, strict subspace of $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$), membership $y(t) \in \mathbb D^{1,2}$ follows from closedness once a uniform bound $\sup_k \EE\int_0^1\abs{D_ry^{(k)}(t)}^2\dd{r} < \infty$ is established. Such a uniform bound follows from a Gronwall argument on \eqref{eq:picard_iter} entirely parallel to the one carried out below for the limit $y$ itself (establish the bound of this lemma's statement at level $k$, with a constant independent of $k$, and pass to the limit using Fatou's lemma). Granting this, closedness of $D$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        y(t) = \lim_{k \to \infty} y^{(k)}(t) \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}, \qquad D_ry^{(k)}(t) \to D_ry(t) \text{ weakly in } \mathcal L^2(\Omega\times(0,1)),
    \end{equation}
    for every $t \in T$.

    We now proceed to prove the bound on the squared expectation of the Malliavin derivative of the solution. Start by squaring the Malliavin derivative \eqref{eq:mall_dev_y} on both sides and taking the expectation. Any cross-terms that involve It\^o integrals vanish since It\^o integrals are martingales with mean $0$. As such, we are left with:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \EE(\abs{D_ry(t)}^2) &= \EE (\abs{K_\alpha(t, r)\sigma(r, y(r))}^2) \\
        &+ 2\EE \left ( K_\alpha(t, r)\sigma(r, y(r))\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s} \right ) \\
        &+ \EE \left ( \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s}}^2 \right ) \\
        &+ \EE \left ( \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{B_s}}^2 \right ).
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    The first term is bounded with the uniform constant $C_\sigma$ from \eqref{eq:Csigma}:
    \begin{equation}
        \EE (\abs{K_\alpha(t, r)\sigma(r, y(r))}^2) = \abs{K_\alpha(t, r)}^2 \EE (\abs{\sigma(t, y(t))}^2) \leq C_\sigma^2 \abs{K_\alpha(t, r)}^2 = O((t - r)^{2\alpha-2}).
    \end{equation}
    For the second term, we apply the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &2\EE \left ( K_\alpha(t, r)\sigma(r, y(r))\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s} \right ) \\
        &\quad \leq 2 \left ( \EE(\abs{K_\alpha(t, r)\sigma(r, y(r))}^2) \right )^{1/2} \left ( \EE \left ( \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s}}^2 \right ) \right )^{1/2}.
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    The first factor is bounded above by $C_\sigma \abs{K_\alpha(t, r)} = O((t - r)^{\alpha - 1})$ from the first term. The second factor is bounded in the third term below.
    
    For the third term, apply the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality within the integral.
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s}}^2 &\leq \left (\int_r^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \dd{s} \right ) \left (\int_r^t \abs{\partial_yb(s, y(s))}^2 \abs{D_ry(s)}^2 \dd{s} \right ).
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    By assumption \ref{as:a4}, $\abs{\partial_yb(s, y(s))} \leq L'$ uniformly in $s \in T$ and $y \in \mathbb{R}$. Taking the expectation, we have
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \EE \left ( \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s}}^2 \right ) &\leq (L')^2 \left (\int_r^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \dd{s} \right ) \int_r^t \EE(\abs{D_ry(s)}^2) \dd{s}.
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    As per Remark~\ref{rem:kernel_l2}, the kernel integral is square-integrable for $\alpha > 1/2$:
    \begin{equation}
        \int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)^2 \dd{s} = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\int_r^t (t - s)^{2(\alpha - 1)} \dd{s} = \frac{(t - r)^{2\alpha - 1}}{(2\alpha - 1)\Gamma(\alpha)^2} = C_K(t - r)^{2\alpha - 1}.
    \end{equation}
    Therefore:
    \begin{equation}
        \EE \left ( \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_yb(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{s}}^2 \right ) \leq C_K(L')^2(t - r)^{2\alpha - 1} \int_r^t \EE(\abs{D_ry(s)}^2) \dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    
    For the fourth term, apply It\^o isometry:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \EE \left ( \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{B_s}}^2 \right ) &= \EE \left ( \int_r^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \abs{\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))}^2 \abs{D_ry(s)}^2 \dd{s} \right ).
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    By \ref{as:a4}, $\abs{\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))} \leq L'$ uniformly:
    \begin{equation}
        \EE \left ( \abs*{\int_r^t K_\alpha(t, s)\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))D_ry(s)\dd{B_s}}^2 \right ) \leq (L')^2 \int_r^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \EE(\abs{D_ry(s)}^2) \dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    
    Define $u(t) = \EE(\abs{D_ry(t)}^2)$ for brevity. Combining all four terms and absorbing the second term into a Gronwall treatment, we obtain the weakly singular Volterra inequality:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:volterra_ineq}
        u(t) \leq a(t) + \tilde C \int_r^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 u(s) \dd{s},
    \end{equation}
    where $a(t) = C_\sigma^2 \abs{K_\alpha(t, r)}^2 = O((t - r)^{2\alpha - 2})$ is the source term and $\tilde C = \max\{C_K(L')^2, (L')^2\}$ is a constant depending on $\alpha$ and $L'$.
    
    Substituting for $K_\alpha(t, s)$ into \eqref{eq:volterra_ineq}, this becomes:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:volterra_explicit}
        u(t) \leq a(t) + \frac{\tilde{C}}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2} \int_r^t (t - s)^{2(\alpha - 1)} u(s) \dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    The kernel $(t-s)^{2(\alpha-1)}$ in \eqref{eq:volterra_explicit} is of the form $(t-s)^{\beta-1}$ with $\beta = 2\alpha - 1 \in (0,1]$ for $\alpha \in (1/2,1]$, not $\beta = 2\alpha-2$: applying Lemma~\ref{lem:conv_gronwall} with this $\beta$ gives an $E_{2\alpha-1}$ Mittag-Leffler factor and $\Gamma(2\alpha-1)$, which is finite and positive throughout $\alpha \in (1/2,1]$ (unlike $\Gamma(2\alpha-2)$, which is negative on this range and undefined at $\alpha=1$). Moreover the source term $a(t) = C_\sigma^2\abs{K_\alpha(t,r)}^2 \propto (t-r)^{2\alpha-2}$ is \emph{decreasing} in $t$ for fixed $r$ (since $2\alpha - 2 < 0$), so the non-decreasing branch of Lemma~\ref{lem:conv_gronwall} does not apply directly. The general weakly-singular Gronwall inequality for a decreasing, algebraically-singular source (Ye--Gao--Ding \cite{YE20071075}, Thm.~1) instead gives, after shifting the integration variable to start at $r$,
    \begin{equation}
        u(t) \leq C_\sigma^2 \abs{K_\alpha(t, r)}^2\,\Gamma(2\alpha-1)\,E_{2\alpha-1,\,2\alpha-1}\!\left ( \frac{\tilde{C}}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\Gamma(2\alpha - 1)(t - r)^{2\alpha - 1} \right ),
    \end{equation}
    where $E_{\beta,\gamma}$ is the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler function of Definition~\ref{def:mittag_leffler}. This two-parameter factor is bounded by an absolute constant $M_\alpha$ depending only on $\alpha$ and $L'$, since $t, r \in T = [0,1]$ are bounded. Therefore:
    \begin{equation}
        \EE(\abs{D_ry(t)}^2) \leq C_D(t - r)^{2\alpha - 2},
    \end{equation}
    where $C_D > 0$ depends on the fractional order $\alpha$, the Lipschitz constant $L'$ from \ref{as:a4}, and the uniform bound $C_\sigma$ from equation \eqref{eq:Csigma}.
\end{proof}

The ML-PIFLNN collocates at $N_c$ points. The following lemma permits the extension of the uniform bounds on this finite set to the entire set $T$.
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:collocation_to_continuum}
    Let $g : T \to \mathbb R$ be H\"older continuous of order $q \in (0, 1]$, with seminorm bounded by $\Theta'$. Suppose
    \begin{equation}
        \frac 1{N_c} \sum_{i=1}^{N_c} g(t_i)^2 \leq \eta^2
    \end{equation}
    where $t_i$ for $i \in \{1, \dots , N_c\}$ are the shifted Chebyshev points \eqref{eq:chebyshev}. Then, there exists a constant $C_q > 0$ depending only on the exponent $q$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} \abs{g(t)} \leq \eta + C_q\Theta'N_c^{-q},
    \end{equation}
    and hence,
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} \abs{g(t)}^2 \leq 2\eta^2 + 2C_q^2(\Theta')^2N_c^{-2q}.
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    We start by constructing the interpolating polynomial $P_{N_c}$ that agrees with $g$ at all the $N_c$ collocation points. This polynomial has degree at most $N_c - 1$.
    
    We utilize Jackson's theorem \cite{devore1993constructive}, to claim that among all polynomials of degree at most $N_c - 1$, there exists a polynomial $P^*$ optimal in the $\mathcal L^\infty$ norm satisfying
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{g - P^*}_\infty \leq C_1 \Theta' N_c^{-q}
    \end{equation}
    for a constant $C_1$ that only depends on the exponent $q$.

    We utilize Lebesgue's lemma \cite{devore1993constructive} to relate the interpolating polynomial $P_{N_c}$ to the optimal approximation $P^*$:
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{g - P_{N_c}}_{\infty} \leq (1 + \Lambda_{N_c})\norm{g - P^*}_\infty,
    \end{equation} 
    where $\Lambda_{N_c}$ is the Lebesgue constant for the chosen nodes. For Chebyshev nodes, the Lebesgue constant grows logarithmically, $\Lambda_{N_c} = O(\log N_c)$ \cite{BOS20137}. Combining the bounds:
    \begin{equation}
        \norm{g - P_{N_c}}_{\infty} \leq (1 + C_l\log N_c) C_1\Theta' N_c^{-q}
    \end{equation}
    for some constant $C_l > 0$. We now utilize the triangle inequality:
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} |g(t)| \leq \eta + \norm{g - P_{N_c}}_\infty \leq \eta + (1 + C_l\log N_c) C_1\Theta' N_c^{-q}.
    \end{equation}
    For any $\epsilon > 0$, since logarithmic factors decay slower than any polynomial rate, we have $(\log N_c) N_c^{-q} = O(N_c^{-q+\epsilon})$. Thus there exists a constant $C_q(\epsilon)$ independent of $N_c$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T}|g(t)| \leq \eta + C_q(\epsilon)\Theta' N_c^{-q+\epsilon}.
    \end{equation}
    Taking $\epsilon$ arbitrarily small and absorbing the logarithmic factor into the constant $C_q$ (at the cost of replacing $q$ by $q - \epsilon$ for any $\epsilon > 0$), we obtain
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:g_bound}
        \sup_{t \in T}|g(t)| \leq \eta + C_q\Theta' N_c^{-q}.
    \end{equation}
    
    The squared bound follows from the inequality $(a+b)^2 \leq 2a^2 + 2b^2$:
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T} \abs{g(t)}^2 \leq 2\eta^2 + 2C_q^2\Theta'^2 N_c^{-2q},
    \end{equation}
    which is the claimed result.
\end{proof}

Definition~\ref{def:trace} attaches a trace to every Malliavin differentiable coefficient vector. We now state, and prove, the identity that this definition serves. We do so at an arbitrary $\mathbf c$, since the scheme subtracts $\tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma]$ while the vector that appears in the error decomposition below is the output of the corrected pass, $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$, and both are instances of the following lemma. Recall the kernel vector, whose $j$th component is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ut}
    (\mathbf u_t)_j(s) = K_\alpha(t, s)M_j^\Lambda(s).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:trace_identity}
    Let $\mathbf c \in \mathbb D^{1, 2}(\mathbb R^{n+1})$ be such that $c_j(\mathbf u_t)_j \in \Dom(\delta)$ for every $j \in \{0, \dots , n\}$ and every $t \in T$, where $\mathbf u_t$ is the kernel vector \eqref{eq:ut}. Then, for every $t \in T$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_identity}
        \mathbf c^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\, \delta B_s + \tau_n[\mathbf c](t) - \mathbf c^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t).
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    Fix $t \in T$ and recall the kernel vector $\mathbf u_t$ of \eqref{eq:ut}, whose $j$th component is $(\mathbf u_t)_j(s) = K_\alpha(t, s)M_j^\Lambda(s)$. Each such component is deterministic, lies in $H$ by Remark~\ref{rem:kernel_l2}, and vanishes for $s \geq t$ by the indicator in \eqref{eq:kernel}. Being deterministic, $\mathbf u_t$ is trivially adapted, so Proposition~\ref{prop:ito_special_case} identifies $\delta((\mathbf u_t)_j)$ with the It\^o integral $I_j(t)$ for every $j$. The projection identity \eqref{eq:projection_identity} therefore reads
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:delta_ut}
        \delta(\mathbf u_t) = \mathbf I_n(t) = \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) + \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t),
    \end{equation}
    understood componentwise.

    Both hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:multiplication_formula} are now in force for the pair $F = c_j$ and $u = (\mathbf u_t)_j$: the former lies in $\mathbb D^{1,2}$ since $\mathbf c \in \mathbb D^{1,2}(\mathbb R^{n+1})$, the latter lies in $\Dom(\delta)$ by the preceding paragraph, and the product $c_j(\mathbf u_t)_j$ lies in $\Dom(\delta)$ by assumption. The multiplication formula \eqref{eq:multiplication_formula} gives
    \begin{equation}
        c_j\,\delta \left ( (\mathbf u_t)_j \right ) = \delta \left ( c_j(\mathbf u_t)_j \right ) + \ip{Dc_j, (\mathbf u_t)_j}_H
    \end{equation}
    for every $j \in \{0, \dots , n\}$. Summing over $j$ and using the linearity of $\delta$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_sum}
        \mathbf c^\top \delta(\mathbf u_t) = \delta \left ( \mathbf c^\top \mathbf u_t \right ) + \sum_{j=0}^n \int_0^1 (D_sc_j)(\mathbf u_t)_j(s)\dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    We treat the two terms on the right of \eqref{eq:trace_sum} in turn.

    For the sum, we interchange the finite summation with the integral and substitute for $(\mathbf u_t)_j$:
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{j=0}^n \int_0^1 (D_sc_j)(\mathbf u_t)_j(s)\dd{s} = \int_0^1 K_\alpha(t, s)\sum_{j=0}^n (D_sc_j)M_j^\Lambda(s)\dd{s} = \int_0^1 K_\alpha(t, s)(D_s\mathbf c)^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    The kernel $K_\alpha(t, s)$ vanishes for $s \geq t$, so the range of integration truncates to $(0, t)$ and the right-hand side is precisely the Malliavin trace $\tau_n[\mathbf c](t)$ of Definition~\ref{def:trace}.

    For the divergence term, we have $\mathbf c^\top \mathbf u_t(\cdot) = K_\alpha(t, \cdot)\mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(\cdot)$ directly from \eqref{eq:ut}, so that
    \begin{equation}
        \delta \left ( \mathbf c^\top \mathbf u_t \right ) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\, \delta B_s,
    \end{equation}
    the integral being understood in the Skorokhod sense of Definition~\ref{def:skorokhod}.

    Substituting these two identities and \eqref{eq:delta_ut} into \eqref{eq:trace_sum} yields
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf c^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) + \mathbf c^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s)\, \delta B_s + \tau_n[\mathbf c](t),
    \end{equation}
    and rearranging gives \eqref{eq:trace_identity}.
\end{proof}

Identity \eqref{eq:trace_identity} is exact at every $n$, and it fixes both the sign and the scope of the correction: the trace is subtracted from the algebraic stochastic term, and what remains is a genuine Skorokhod integral of the finite-dimensional diffusion approximation, up to the projection remainder $\boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t)$, which is a separate source of error unrelated to adaptedness. Taking $\mathbf c = \mathbf c^*_\sigma$ recovers the identity for the term $A_n(t)$ of \eqref{eq:An} that the uncorrected pass forms, and taking $\mathbf c = \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ gives the identity we require below.

The integrability hypothesis of the lemma is met in both of these cases. The components of $\mathbf u_t$ are deterministic elements of $H$, so each $\delta((\mathbf u_t)_j) = I_j(t)$ is Gaussian and possesses moments of every order. Under \ref{as:a6} we have $\abs{\mathbf c} \leq \Theta$ a.s.\ for $\mathbf c \in \{\mathbf c^*_\sigma, \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma\}$, which places $c_j\delta((\mathbf u_t)_j)$ in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$, and $\mathbf c \in \mathbb D^{1,2}(\mathbb R^{n+1})$ with the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality \cite{rudin1991functional} places $\ip{Dc_j, (\mathbf u_t)_j}_H$ there as well. These are exactly the conditions under which $c_j(\mathbf u_t)_j \in \Dom(\delta)$ \cite{nualart2006malliavin}. For $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$, membership in $\mathbb D^{1,2}(\mathbb R^{n+1})$ is Proposition~\ref{prop:Dtheta}.

We also are interested in the difference between the traces of $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$ and $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$. We quantify this defect for $t \in T$ as follows.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_defect}
    \Delta_n(t) = \tau_n[\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t), \quad \varepsilon^2_\Delta = \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\Delta_n(t)}^2).
\end{equation}

We now seek to provide an exact representation for our error.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:error_rep}
    Let $R(t)$ denote the continuous corrected residual
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:corrected_residual}
        R(t) = \hat{y}_n(t) - y_0 - \hat{\mathbf c}_b^\top \mathbf P_\alpha(t) \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) - \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) + \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t),
    \end{equation}
    whose restriction to the collocation set $T_c$ is exactly $\hat{\mathbf r}_1$ from \eqref{eq:gn_corrected_residual}. Under \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a7}, the global error $e(t) = \hat{y}_n(t) - y(t)$ admits, for every $t \in T$, the exact decomposition
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:error_decomposition}
    \begin{aligned}
        e(t) &= \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\xi_b(s)\dd{s} \\
        &+ \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(b(s, \hat{y}_n(s)) - b(s, y(s)))\dd{s} \\
        &+ \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(\hat{\sigma}(s) - \sigma(s, y(s))) \, \delta B_s \\
        &+  d(t)
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:defect}
        d(t) = R(t) + \Delta_n(t) - \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t).
    \end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We start by rewriting the two operational terms of \eqref{eq:corrected_residual} as the integrals they are built to represent. By Lemma~\ref{lem:P_exact} applied to $\hat{\mathbf c}_b$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:P_exact_hat}
        \hat{\mathbf c}_b^\top \mathbf P_\alpha(t)\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\hat b(s)\dd{s}
    \end{equation}
    with no truncation error, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:trace_identity} applied to $\mathbf c = \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_identity_hat}
        \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\hat \sigma(s)\, \delta B_s + \tau_n[\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma](t) - \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t).
    \end{equation}

    Substituting \eqref{eq:P_exact_hat} and \eqref{eq:trace_identity_hat} into \eqref{eq:corrected_residual} and solving for $\hat y_n(t)$, we obtain
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:hat_y_exact}
        \hat y_n(t) = y_0 + \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\hat b(s)\dd{s} + \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\hat \sigma(s)\, \delta B_s + R(t) + \Delta_n(t) - \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top \boldsymbol \varepsilon_n(t),
    \end{equation}
    where the two trace terms have combined into $\tau_n[\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t) = \Delta_n(t)$ by \eqref{eq:trace_defect}. Equation~\eqref{eq:hat_y_exact} is the exact continuous-time equation satisfied by the output of the corrected pass. It is the SVIE \eqref{eq:svie} with the It\^o integral replaced by a Skorokhod integral, the coefficient functions replaced by their projections, and three explicit defects appended.

    We now subtract the exact solution. The map $s \mapsto K_\alpha(t, s)\sigma(s, y(s))$ is adapted, and it lies in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega \times (0, 1))$ by \ref{as:a3} together with Remark~\ref{rem:kernel_l2}, so Proposition~\ref{prop:ito_special_case} permits us to read the stochastic term of the SVIE \eqref{eq:svie} as a Skorokhod integral:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:svie_skorokhod}
        y(t) = y_0 + \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)b(s, y(s))\dd{s} + \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\sigma(s, y(s))\, \delta B_s.
    \end{equation}
    Subtracting \eqref{eq:svie_skorokhod} from \eqref{eq:hat_y_exact}, the initial conditions cancel, and the linearity of $\delta$ permits us to combine the two stochastic terms into one:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:error_prelim}
        e(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s) \left ( \hat b(s) - b(s, y(s)) \right )\dd{s} + \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s) \left ( \hat \sigma(s) - \sigma(s, y(s)) \right )\, \delta B_s + d(t),
    \end{equation}
    with $d(t)$ exactly as in \eqref{eq:defect}.

    It remains to split the drift integrand. By the definition \eqref{eq:xi_deviations} of $\xi_b$, we add and subtract $b(s, \hat y_n(s))$ to obtain
    \begin{equation}
        \hat b(s) - b(s, y(s)) = \xi_b(s) + \left ( b(s, \hat y_n(s)) - b(s, y(s)) \right ),
    \end{equation}
    which separates the first integral of \eqref{eq:error_prelim} into the first two terms of \eqref{eq:error_decomposition}.
\end{proof}

We note two features of the decomposition \eqref{eq:error_decomposition}, since they are precisely what an uncorrected argument would obscure. The third term is a Skorokhod integral and cannot be bounded by the It\^o isometry, and the trace has disappeared from the decomposition entirely except through $\Delta_n$, which is the whole point of subtracting it, but which is controlled neither by the training loss, nor by $n$, nor by $N_c$.

The trace defect $\Delta_n$ arises because the two passes solve for different coefficient vectors: the first pass finds $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$ by minimizing the uncorrected loss, and the second pass finds $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ by applying a Gauss--Newton correction. The key observation is that the two solutions differ by a controlled amount, and the Malliavin derivative of the trace operator is uniformly bounded, so their traces also differ in a controlled way.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:trace_lipschitz}
    Let $\mathbf c, \mathbf c' \in \mathbb D^{1,2}(\mathbb R^{n+1})$ satisfy $\abs{\mathbf c}, \abs{\mathbf c'} \leq \Theta$ a.s.\ (as guaranteed by Assumption~\ref{as:a6}). Then there exists a constant $L_\tau > 0$, depending on $\Theta$, $\alpha$, and the bounds on the Müntz--Legendre basis, such that
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:trace_lipschitz}
    \begin{aligned}
        \sup_{t \in T} \EE(\abs{\tau_n[\mathbf c](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c'](t)}^2) &\leq L_\tau \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \abs{\mathbf c - \mathbf c'} (D_s \mathbf c)^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s} \right )^2  \\
        &+ L_\tau \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \abs{\mathbf c - \mathbf c'} (D_s \mathbf c')^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s} \right )^2.
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    By Definition~\ref{def:trace}, we have for each $t \in T$,
    \begin{equation}
        \tau_n[\mathbf c](t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(D_s \mathbf c)^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s}, \quad \tau_n[\mathbf c'](t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(D_s \mathbf c')^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    Thus
    \begin{equation}
        \tau_n[\mathbf c](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c'](t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(D_s (\mathbf c - \mathbf c'))^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    Squaring and taking expectations, then applying the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality to the integral, we obtain
    \begin{equation}
        \EE(\abs{\tau_n[\mathbf c](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c'](t)}^2) \leq C_K \EE \left ( \int_0^t (D_s(\mathbf c - \mathbf c'))^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(s) \dd{s} \right )^2,
    \end{equation}
    where $C_K$ depends on $K_\alpha$ and the Müntz--Legendre basis $\mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t)$. Expanding $(D_s(\mathbf c - \mathbf c'))^\top = (D_s \mathbf c)^\top - (D_s \mathbf c')^\top$ and applying the triangle inequality gives the stated bound in terms of two integrals, each involving the difference $\mathbf c - \mathbf c'$ and one of the Malliavin derivatives.
\end{proof}

The second step is to bound the proximity of $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ and $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$ themselves. Recall from \S\ref{sec:collocation_loss} that $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$ minimizes the uncorrected loss
\begin{equation}
    L_{\mathrm{SDE}} = \frac 1{N_c}\sum_{i=1}^{N_c} \left ( \mathbf c^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - y_0\mathbf e^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - \mathbf c_b^\top \mathbf P_\alpha(t_i) \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) - \mathbf c_\sigma^\top \mathbf S_\alpha \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(t_i) \right )^2 + L_b + L_\sigma,
\end{equation}
while $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ minimizes the same loss with the SDE block corrected by subtracting $\tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma]$ from the target, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf r}_1 = \mathbf r_1 + \boldsymbol \tau,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol \tau = [\tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t_1), \ldots, \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t_{N_c})]^\top$ is frozen at the values from the first pass. Both minimizers satisfy first-order optimality conditions that differ by the residual adjustment $\boldsymbol \tau$. By a standard argument in perturbation theory for Gauss--Newton systems \cite{nualart2006malliavin}, if $\mathbf H$ denotes the Hessian of the loss at $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$, then pathwise
\begin{equation}
    \abs{\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma - \mathbf c^*_\sigma}^2 \leq \norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}^2 \abs{\boldsymbol \tau}^2.
\end{equation}
Since $\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}$ is itself a random variable and not a deterministic constant, we cannot pull it outside the expectation. Instead we apply H\"older's inequality with the exponent pair $(p/2, p/(p-2))$ furnished by the moment $p>2$ of Assumption~\ref{as:a5},
\begin{equation}
    \EE(\abs{\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma - \mathbf c^*_\sigma}^2) \leq \EE(\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}^2\abs{\boldsymbol \tau}^2) \leq \left ( \EE(\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}^p) \right )^{2/p} \left ( \EE(\abs{\boldsymbol \tau}^{2p/(p-2)}) \right )^{(p-2)/p},
\end{equation}
which is finite provided $\boldsymbol \tau = \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma]$ possesses a finite moment of order $2p/(p-2)$. We add this as an explicit requirement to Assumption~\ref{as:a6} alongside the second moment already assumed there. Abbreviating the right-hand side as $\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}_p^2\,\norm{\boldsymbol\tau}_{2p/(p-2)}^2$, this gives the working bound
\begin{equation}
    \EE(\abs{\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma - \mathbf c^*_\sigma}^2) \leq C_H' \, \EE(\abs{\tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma]}^2)^{(p-2)/p},
\end{equation}
for a constant $C_H'$ depending on $p$ and the moment bounds of Assumption~\ref{as:a5}--\ref{as:a6}, which we use below in place of the pathwise-deterministic version.

Combining Lemma~\ref{lem:trace_lipschitz} with this proximity bound yields the following.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:trace_defect_bound}
    Under Assumptions~\ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a7}, the trace defect $\Delta_n(t) = \tau_n[\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t)$ defined in \eqref{eq:trace_defect} satisfies
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Delta_bound}
        \varepsilon^2_\Delta = \sup_{t \in T} \EE(\abs{\Delta_n(t)}^2) \leq C_\Delta,
    \end{equation}
    where the constant $C_\Delta$ depends on $\Theta$, the Lipschitz moduli of $b$ and $\sigma$ (from \ref{as:a2}), the smoothness constant from \ref{as:a4}, the Hessian invertibility from \ref{as:a5}, and the regularity of the fractional order $\alpha$, but \emph{not} on $n$. This is an $O(1)$ bound: the two-pass scheme of \S\ref{sec:proposed_method} applies the trace correction exactly once, using the trace $\tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma]$ frozen at the uncorrected minimizer, and nothing in the argument below forces $\Delta_n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. Corollary~\ref{cor:fixed_point_correction} shows that iterating the correction to a fixed point instead drives $\varepsilon_\Delta$ to $0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    By Definition~\ref{def:trace}, the Malliavin derivative of the trace satisfies
    \begin{equation}
        D_s(\Delta_n(t)) = D_s(\tau_n[\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma](t)) - D_s(\tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma](t)) = \int_s^t K_\alpha(t, u)(D_s D_u \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma - D_s D_u \mathbf c^*_\sigma)^\top \mathbf M_n^\Lambda(u) \dd{u}.
    \end{equation}
    The Malliavin--Sobolev norm of $\Delta_n$ is, by Definition~\eqref{eq:mal_sob_norm},
    \begin{align}
        \norm{\Delta_n}_{\mathbb D^{1,2}}^2 &= \EE(\abs{\Delta_n}^2) + \EE\left(\int_0^1 \abs{D_s \Delta_n}^2 \dd{s}\right).
    \end{align}
    We bound the derivative term. The value term $\EE(\abs{\Delta_n}^2)$, which the Malliavin--Sobolev norm bounds only jointly with the derivative term and not separately, is controlled instead by the Lipschitz proximity bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:trace_lipschitz} referenced above. For the derivative term,
    \begin{equation}
        \EE\left(\int_0^1 \abs{D_s \Delta_n}^2 \dd{s}\right) \leq C_K \EE\left(\int_0^1 \abs{D_s(\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma - \mathbf c^*_\sigma)}^2 \dd{s}\right),
    \end{equation}
    where $C_K$ absorbs the bounded variation and integrability of $K_\alpha$ from Remark~\ref{rem:kernel_l2} and the $\mathcal L^\infty$ norm of the basis functions.

By Assumption~\ref{as:a5b}, which we stated to cover both the uncorrected minimizer $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:Dtheta} and the corrected minimizer $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ precisely so that it could be invoked here, $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma\in\mathbb D^{1,2}$ with its Malliavin derivative given by the analogous implicit-function formula, and is in particular, like $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$, a Lipschitz function of the trace-adjusted target with Lipschitz constant controlled by $\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}$. Granting this,
    \begin{equation}
        \EE\left(\int_0^1 \abs{D_s(\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma - \mathbf c^*_\sigma)}^2 \dd{s}\right) \leq C_H \EE\left(\int_0^1 \abs{D_s \boldsymbol \tau}^2 \dd{s}\right),
    \end{equation}
    where $C_H = \EE(\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}^2)$ is finite by Assumption~\ref{as:a5}. The Malliavin derivative of $\boldsymbol \tau = \tau_n[\mathbf c^*_\sigma]$ is computed from Proposition~\ref{prop:Dtheta}, giving
    \begin{equation}
        \EE\left(\int_0^1 \abs{D_s \boldsymbol \tau}^2 \dd{s}\right) \leq C_D \EE(\abs{\mathbf c^*_\sigma}^2) \leq C_D \Theta^2,
    \end{equation}
    using the boundedness of $\mathbf c^*_\sigma$ from Assumption~\ref{as:a6}. This bounds the derivative term by $C_KC_HC_D\Theta^2$, a constant independent of $n$. Combined with Lemma~\ref{lem:trace_lipschitz}'s bound on the value term, we arrive at
    \begin{equation}
        \sup_{t \in T}\EE(\abs{\Delta_n(t)}^2) \leq C_\Delta,
    \end{equation}
    where $C_\Delta$ collects the dependencies on the fractional order, the bounds on the coefficients, and the Hessian condition number. Nothing in this chain decays in $n$: both inputs, $\Theta^2$ and the Lipschitz constants, are $n$-uniform by \ref{as:a6}.
\end{proof}
\subsection{Proof of convergence}
We now finally embark on the proof of convergence. Every ingredient is in place: Proposition~\ref{prop:error_rep} expresses the global error $e(t) = \hat y_n(t) - y(t)$ as a sum of four explicitly identified terms, and each of the four is controlled by a quantity we have already introduced. The drift terms are controlled by the training loss and the Lipschitz hypothesis \ref{as:a2}. The stochastic term is controlled by the Skorokhod isometry together with the Malliavin consistency defect $\varepsilon_D(n)$ of \ref{as:a7}. And the residual block is controlled by the training loss, the frozen-trace defect $\varepsilon_\Delta$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:trace_defect_bound}, and the projection remainder $\delta_S(n)$. What is left to do is to bound the four terms, add them, and observe that the two error terms carrying the solution itself assemble into a weakly singular Volterra inequality, which the generalized Gronwall inequality of Lemma~\ref{lem:conv_gronwall} closes.

Two elementary consequences of Remark~\ref{rem:kernel_l2} are used repeatedly, and we record them once. For every $t \in T$ and $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel_bounds}
    \int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \dd{s} = \frac{t^{2\alpha - 1}}{(2\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha)^2} \leq c_\alpha^2, \quad c_\alpha = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)\sqrt{2\alpha - 1}},
\end{equation}
and, integrating the kernel rather than its square,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel_l1}
    \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\dd{s} = \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^t (t-s)^{\alpha - 1}\dd{s} = \frac{t^\alpha}{\alpha \Gamma(\alpha)} = \frac{t^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha + 1)} \leq \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}.
\end{equation}
The first is finite precisely because $\alpha > 1/2$, and it is the same inequality that made the stochastic operational matrix well defined in the first place. The second is finite for every $\alpha > 0$. Both bounds are attained at $t = 1$, since $T = [0, 1]$.

Before proceeding, we verify that the Malliavin consistency defect of \ref{as:a7} is a finite quantity for each fixed $n$, so that the assumption constrains only its rate of decay and does not smuggle in a finiteness claim.
\begin{remark}\label{rem:epsD_finite}
    Fix $n$. Splitting the integrand of $\varepsilon_D(n)^2$ with the elementary inequality $\abs{x - z}^2 \leq 2\abs{x}^2 + 2\abs{z}^2$, we obtain
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \varepsilon_D(n)^2 &\leq 2\sup_{t \in T}\int_0^1 \int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \EE (\abs{D_r\hat\sigma(s)}^2)\dd{s}\dd{r} \\
        &+ 2\sup_{t \in T}\int_0^1 \int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2\EE (\abs{\partial_y \sigma(s, y(s))D_ry(s)}^2)\dd{s}\dd{r}.
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    The first supremum is finite by \ref{as:a7}(a). For the second, \ref{as:a4} gives $\abs{\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))} \leq L'$ uniformly, and Lemma~\ref{lem:mall_dev_y} gives $\EE(\abs{D_ry(s)}^2) \leq C_D(s - r)^{2\alpha - 2}$ for $r < s$, together with $D_ry(s) = 0$ for $r > s$. Interchanging the order of integration by Tonelli's theorem \cite{folland1999real}, which is permissible since the integrand is non-negative, and performing the $r$ integral first over its effective range $(0, s)$,
    \begin{equation}
        \int_0^s (s - r)^{2\alpha - 2}\dd{r} = \frac{s^{2\alpha - 1}}{2\alpha - 1} \leq \frac 1{2\alpha - 1},
    \end{equation}
    which is finite precisely because $2\alpha - 2 > -1$. Feeding this back and using \eqref{eq:kernel_bounds},
    \begin{equation}
        \int_0^1 \int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2 \EE (\abs{\partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))D_ry(s)}^2)\dd{s}\dd{r} \leq \frac{(L')^2C_D}{2\alpha - 1}\int_0^t \abs{K_\alpha(t, s)}^2\dd{s} \leq \frac{(L')^2C_D}{(2\alpha-1)^2\Gamma(\alpha)^2}.
    \end{equation}
    Hence $\varepsilon_D(n) < \infty$ for every $n$, and \ref{as:a7}(b) is a statement about its decay alone.
\end{remark}

We can now state and prove the main result of this section.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:convergence}
    Suppose \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a7} hold, and suppose the corrected pass terminates at $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = [\hat{\mathbf c}^\top \; \hat{\mathbf c}_b^\top \; \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top]^\top$ with a corrected training loss no larger than a tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$, almost surely:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:corrected_loss}
        \hat L(\hat{\mathbf c}, \hat{\mathbf c}_b, \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma) = \hat L_{\mathrm{SDE}} + \lambda_bL_b + \lambda_\sigma L_\sigma \leq \varepsilon, \quad \hat L_{\mathrm{SDE}} = \frac 1{N_c}\sum_{i=1}^{N_c}\abs{R(t_i)}^2,
    \end{equation}
    where $R$ is the continuous corrected residual \eqref{eq:corrected_residual}, whose restriction to $T_c$ is the corrected residual block $\hat{\mathbf r}_1$ of \eqref{eq:gn_corrected_residual}, and $L_b$, $L_\sigma$ are the projection losses \eqref{eq:loss_components} evaluated at $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$. Set
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:eps_defs}
        \varepsilon_R^2 = 2\varepsilon + 2C_q^2(\Theta')^2N_c^{-2q}, \quad \varepsilon_b^2 = \frac{2\varepsilon}{\lambda_b} + 2C_q^2(\Theta')^2N_c^{-2q}, \quad \varepsilon_\sigma^2 = \frac{2\varepsilon}{\lambda_\sigma} + 2C_q^2(\Theta')^2N_c^{-2q}.
    \end{equation}
    Then
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:main_bound}
        \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\hat y_n(t) - y(t)}^2) \leq M_\alpha \Psi,
    \end{equation}
    where the forcing term is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Psi}
        \Psi = \frac{4\varepsilon_b^2}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)^2} + \frac{8\varepsilon_\sigma^2}{(2\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha)^2} + 4\varepsilon_D(n)^2 + 12 \left ( \varepsilon_R^2 + \varepsilon_\Delta^2 + \Theta^2\delta_S(n)^2 \right )
    \end{equation}
    and the amplification factor is
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:Malpha}
        M_\alpha = E_{2\alpha-1}(C_G\Gamma(2\alpha-1)), \quad C_G = \frac{4L^2}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\left ( 2 + \frac 1\alpha \right ).
    \end{equation}
    Consequently, there exists a constant $C_\star > 0$ depending only on $\alpha$, $L$, $\Theta$, $\Theta'$, $C_q$, $\lambda_b$, and $\lambda_\sigma$, and in particular independent of the truncation degree $n$, the collocation count $N_c$, and the tolerance $\varepsilon$, such that
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:five_term_rate}
        \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\hat y_n(t) - y(t)}^2) \leq C_\star \left ( \varepsilon + N_c^{-2q} + \delta_S(n)^2 + \varepsilon_D(n)^2 + \varepsilon_\Delta^2 \right ).
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Write $e(t) = \hat y_n(t) - y(t)$ for the global error and $\hat e(t) = \EE(\abs{e(t)}^2)$ for its second moment. The latter is finite for every $t \in T$ since the exact solution lies in $\mathcal C(T; \mathcal L^2(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP))$ by well-posedness, and the fitted solution is a finite linear combination of bounded basis functions with coefficients bounded almost surely by \ref{as:a6}. Both solutions are mean-square continuous, so all integrals are well defined.

    The decomposition \eqref{eq:error_decomposition} expresses the error as a sum of four terms:
    \begin{equation}
        T_1(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\xi_b(s)\dd{s}, \quad T_2(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(b(s, \hat y_n(s)) - b(s, y(s)))\dd{s},
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        T_3(t) = \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)(\hat \sigma(s) - \sigma(s, y(s)))\, \delta B_s, \quad T_4(t) = R(t) + \Delta_n(t) - \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma^\top \boldsymbol\varepsilon_n(t).
    \end{equation}
    
    The three summands of the corrected loss \eqref{eq:corrected_loss} are each non-negative, so the single hypothesis $\hat L \leq \varepsilon$ bounds all three separately. The three loss terms give discrete bounds on $R$, $\xi_b$, and $\xi_\sigma$ at the collocation points. Each of these functions is Hölder continuous of order $q$ on $T$ with seminorm bounded by a fixed multiple of $\Theta'$ by \ref{as:a6}: for $\xi_b$ and $\xi_\sigma$ this is immediate, while for $R$ it follows because its other components are either projections of the listed H\"older functions or fractional integrals of them. By Lemma~\ref{lem:collocation_to_continuum} applied pathwise to each, the collocation bounds extend to uniform bounds on $T$:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:step1_bounds}
        \sup_{t \in T}\EE (\abs{R(t)}^2) \leq \varepsilon_R^2, \quad \sup_{t \in T}\EE (\abs{\xi_b(t)}^2) \leq \varepsilon_b^2, \quad \sup_{t \in T}\EE (\abs{\xi_\sigma(t)}^2) \leq \varepsilon_\sigma^2,
    \end{equation}
    with $\varepsilon_R$, $\varepsilon_b$, $\varepsilon_\sigma$ as in \eqref{eq:eps_defs}. This is the only place the training loss is used. It buys control of the coefficients in value everywhere on $T$, not at derivative level, which is why \ref{as:a7}(b) must be assumed.

    Both $T_1$ and $T_2$ are ordinary integrals against the kernel. For $T_1$, Cauchy--Schwarz splitting the kernel as $K_\alpha = K_\alpha^{1/2}\cdot K_\alpha^{1/2}$ gives
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{T_1(t)}^2 \leq \left ( \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\dd{s} \right ) \left ( \int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\abs{\xi_b(s)}^2\dd{s} \right ) \leq \frac 1{\Gamma(\alpha+1)^2}\sup_{s \in T}\abs{\xi_b(s)}^2,
    \end{equation}
    using \eqref{eq:kernel_l1} twice. Taking expectations gives
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:T1_bound}
        \EE (\abs{T_1(t)}^2) \leq \frac{\varepsilon_b^2}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)^2}.
    \end{equation}
    For $T_2$, the same approach applies but the integrand carries the error. By \ref{as:a2}, $\abs{b(s, \hat y_n(s)) - b(s, y(s))} \leq L\abs{e(s)}$, so
    \begin{equation}
        \abs{T_2(t)}^2 \leq \frac{L^2}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\int_0^t K_\alpha(t, s)\abs{e(s)}^2\dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    By Tonelli, the expectation interchanges with the time integral, giving
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:T2_bound}
        \EE (\abs{T_2(t)}^2) \leq \frac{L^2}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\int_0^t(t - s)^{\alpha - 1}\hat e(s)\dd{s}.
    \end{equation}
    This term returns the unknown $\hat e$ under a weakly singular integral. The Gronwall inequality will close this.

    For the stochastic integral $T_3$, the fitted coefficient $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ is anticipating, so the integrand $\hat\sigma(s) - \sigma(s,y(s))$ is not adapted. The integral is a Skorokhod integral, and the Skorokhod isometry of Lemma~\ref{lem:sko_iso} applies. To see that the hypothesis $v \in \mathbb D^{1,2}(\mathcal L^2(0,1))$ holds, observe that the kernel is deterministic and square-integrable, $\hat \sigma \in \mathbb L^{1,2}$ by \ref{as:a7}, and $\sigma(\cdot, y(\cdot))$ is Malliavin differentiable by Lemma~\ref{lem:mall_dev_y}. The isometry gives
    \begin{equation}
        \EE (\abs{T_3(t)}^2) \leq \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \abs{K_\alpha(t,s)(\hat\sigma(s)-\sigma(s,y(s)))}^2\dd{s} \right ) + \EE \left ( \int_0^1\int_0^1 \abs{D_r v_s}^2\dd{s}\dd{r} \right ),
    \end{equation}
    where $v_s = K_\alpha(t,s)(\hat\sigma(s)-\sigma(s,y(s)))$. 

    For the value term, decompose $\hat\sigma(s) - \sigma(s, y(s)) = \xi_\sigma(s) + (\sigma(s, \hat y_n(s)) - \sigma(s, y(s)))$. By \eqref{eq:step1_bounds}, Lipschitz continuity of $\sigma$, and the kernel bound \eqref{eq:kernel_bounds},
    \begin{equation}
        \EE \left ( \int_0^1 \abs{v_s}^2\dd{s} \right ) \leq \frac{2\varepsilon_\sigma^2}{(2\alpha - 1)\Gamma(\alpha)^2} + \frac{2L^2}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\int_0^t (t - s)^{2\alpha - 2}\hat e(s)\dd{s}.
    \end{equation}

    For the derivative term, differentiate: $D_rv_s = K_\alpha(t, s)(D_r\hat\sigma(s) - \partial_y\sigma(s, y(s))D_ry(s))$ by the chain rule. The double integral is exactly the one defining the Malliavin consistency defect in \ref{as:a7}(b), so
    \begin{equation}
        \EE \left ( \int_0^1\int_0^1\abs{D_rv_s}^2\dd{s}\dd{r} \right ) \leq \varepsilon_D(n)^2.
    \end{equation}
    Combining both terms,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:T3_bound}
        \EE (\abs{T_3(t)}^2) \leq \frac{2\varepsilon_\sigma^2}{(2\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha)^2} + \frac{2L^2}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\int_0^t (t-s)^{2\alpha-2}\hat e(s)\dd{s} + \varepsilon_D(n)^2.
    \end{equation}

    For $T_4$, the last term of \eqref{eq:error_decomposition} is a sum of three quantities: $R$, $\Delta_n$, and the projection remainder. These are bounded by \eqref{eq:step1_bounds}, \eqref{eq:trace_defect}, and Lemma~\ref{lem:proj_remainder} respectively, giving
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:T4_bound}
        \EE (\abs{T_4(t)}^2) \leq 3 \left ( \varepsilon_R^2 + \varepsilon_\Delta^2 + \Theta^2\delta_S(n)^2 \right ).
    \end{equation}
    None of these depends on $\hat e$, so this term contributes only to the deterministic forcing.

    Now assemble all four terms. Squaring $e = T_1 + T_2 + T_3 + T_4$ and taking expectations via Cauchy--Schwarz,
    \begin{equation}
        \hat e(t) \leq \Psi + \frac{4L^2}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\int_0^t (t - s)^{\alpha - 1}\hat e(s)\dd{s} + \frac{8L^2}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\int_0^t (t - s)^{2\alpha - 2}\hat e(s)\dd{s},
    \end{equation}
    where $\Psi$ is given by \eqref{eq:Psi}. Since $2\alpha - 2 \leq \alpha - 1$ for $\alpha \in (1/2, 1]$, we can replace the first kernel by the second:
    \begin{equation}
        \hat e(t) \leq \Psi + C_G\int_0^t (t - s)^{2\alpha - 2}\hat e(s)\dd{s}, \quad C_G = \frac{4L^2}{\Gamma(\alpha)^2}\left ( 2 + \frac 1\alpha \right ),
    \end{equation}
    which is the weakly singular Volterra inequality with exponent $\beta = 2\alpha - 1 \in (0,1]$. Since $\Psi$ is constant (non-decreasing), Lemma~\ref{lem:conv_gronwall} yields
    \begin{equation}
        \hat e(t) \leq \Psi E_{2\alpha-1}(C_G\Gamma(2\alpha-1)t^{2\alpha-1}) \leq \Psi E_{2\alpha-1}(C_G\Gamma(2\alpha-1)) =: M_\alpha\Psi
    \end{equation}
    uniformly in $t \in T$. This gives \eqref{eq:main_bound}.

    To simplify to \eqref{eq:five_term_rate}, substitute \eqref{eq:eps_defs} into \eqref{eq:Psi}. Each $\varepsilon_R^2$, $\varepsilon_b^2$, $\varepsilon_\sigma^2$ splits into a term proportional to $\varepsilon$ and a term proportional to $N_c^{-2q}$, with coefficients depending only on $\alpha$, $\lambda_b$, $\lambda_\sigma$, $C_q$, and $\Theta'$. Collecting like terms and multiplying by $M_\alpha$ gives \eqref{eq:five_term_rate}, where the constant $C_\star$ depends on the listed parameters but not on $n$, $N_c$, or $\varepsilon$.
\end{proof}

The bound exhibits the five sources of error: optimizer error $\varepsilon$ (how well the solver minimizes the corrected loss), collocation error $N_c^{-2p}$ (how well discrete constraints extend to $T$), projection error $\delta_S(n)^2$ of the stochastic operational matrix, the Malliavin consistency defect $\varepsilon_D(n)^2$ assumed in \ref{as:a7}(b), and the frozen-trace defect $\varepsilon_\Delta^2$ inherent to the two-pass architecture. By Proposition~\ref{prop:trace_defect_bound}, $\varepsilon_\Delta^2$ is $O(1)$ under the fixed, single-correction scheme of Algorithm~\ref{alg:mlpiflnn}: applying $\tau_n[\cdot]$ once does not by itself force $\varepsilon_\Delta \to 0$ as $n\to\infty$, so \eqref{eq:five_term_rate} does not, as it stands, establish convergence. We remove this obstruction next by iterating the correction to a fixed point.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:fixed_point_correction}
    Define the correction map $\mathcal T : \mathbf c_\sigma \mapsto \hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma(\mathbf c_\sigma)$, where $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma(\mathbf c_\sigma)$ re-solves the collocation system with the SDE block target adjusted by $-\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma]$, and consider the Picard sequence $\mathbf c_\sigma^{(0)} = \mathbf c^*_\sigma$, $\mathbf c_\sigma^{(m+1)} = \mathcal T(\mathbf c_\sigma^{(m)})$. Suppose that, in addition to \ref{as:a1}--\ref{as:a7}, $\mathcal T$ is a.s.\ a contraction on the ball $\{\mathbf c_\sigma : \abs{\mathbf c_\sigma}\le\Theta\}$ with a random Lipschitz constant $L_\mathcal T = C_H' \norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}_p L_\tau$ (with $L_\tau$ the Lipschitz constant of Lemma~\ref{lem:trace_lipschitz} and $C_H'\norm{\mathbf H^{-1}}_p$ as in the bound preceding \eqref{eq:d_corrected}) satisfying $\EE(L_\mathcal T^2) < 1$. Then $\mathbf c_\sigma^{(m)}$ converges in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$, as $m\to\infty$, to a random fixed point $\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty$ with $\mathcal T(\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty) = \mathbf c_\sigma^\infty$, i.e.\ $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty]$ is exactly the trace subtracted to produce $\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty$ itself (see the proof for why this makes the corresponding trace defect vanish identically), so that $\varepsilon_\Delta = 0$ in \eqref{eq:five_term_rate}. Consequently, replacing $\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma$ by $\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty$ throughout \S\ref{sec:convergence} and Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence} yields the four-term rate
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:four_term_rate}
        \sup_{t \in T} \EE (\abs{\hat y_n(t) - y(t)}^2) \leq C_\star \left ( \varepsilon + N_c^{-2q} + \delta_S(n)^2 + \varepsilon_D(n)^2 \right ),
    \end{equation}
    which is a genuine convergence statement as $n, N_c \to \infty$ and $\varepsilon \to 0$, subject to $\delta_S(n)\to 0$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:proj_remainder}) and to \ref{as:a7}(b).
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
    $\mathcal T$ maps the a.s.-bounded ball $\{\abs{\mathbf c_\sigma}\le\Theta\}$ into itself by Assumption~\ref{as:a6}, and is a.s.\ a contraction there by hypothesis. The classical Banach fixed-point theorem \cite{rudin1976principles}, applied pathwise for a.e.\ $\omega$, gives a.s.\ convergence of $\mathbf c_\sigma^{(m)}(\omega)$ to a unique fixed point $\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty(\omega)$ in that ball. Convergence in $\mathcal L^2(\Omega)$ follows from the a.s.\ bound $\abs{\mathbf c_\sigma^{(m)}-\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty} \le L_\mathcal T^m\abs{\mathbf c_\sigma^{(0)}-\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty} \le 2\Theta L_\mathcal T^m$, dominated convergence \cite{folland1999real}, and $\EE(L_\mathcal T^{2m})\to 0$, which holds because $\EE(L_\mathcal T^2)<1$ together with H\"older's inequality controls $\EE(L_\mathcal T^{2m})$ for every $m\ge 1$ (e.g.\ via $L_\mathcal T\le 1$ a.s.\ on the event where the contraction hypothesis holds, so $L_\mathcal T^{2m}\le L_\mathcal T^2$ pointwise and dominated convergence applies directly). At the fixed point, $\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty = \mathcal T(\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty)$ is precisely the assertion that re-solving with target adjusted by $\tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty]$ reproduces $\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty$ itself, i.e.\ the coefficient the trace is subtracted \emph{from} and the coefficient the trace is subtracted \emph{to produce} coincide. Substituting $\mathcal T(\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty) = \mathbf c_\sigma^\infty$ into the defect definition~\eqref{eq:trace_defect} therefore gives $\Delta_n^\infty(t) := \tau_n[\mathcal T(\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty)](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty](t) = \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty](t) - \tau_n[\mathbf c_\sigma^\infty](t) \equiv 0$. Substituting $\varepsilon_\Delta = 0$ into \eqref{eq:five_term_rate} gives \eqref{eq:four_term_rate}.
\end{proof}

We emphasize that Corollary~\ref{cor:fixed_point_correction}'s contraction hypothesis, $\EE(L_\mathcal T^2)<1$, is exactly as strong as it looks: establishing it analytically would require a uniform-in-$n$ lower bound on the Hessian's smallest eigenvalue that we have not proved, and it has not been checked numerically either. Algorithm~\ref{alg:mlpiflnn} and every result in \S\ref{sec:numerics} implement only the single-correction scheme of Proposition~\ref{prop:trace_defect_bound} ($\hat{\mathbf c}_\sigma = \mathbf c_\sigma^{(1)}$ above), for which \eqref{eq:five_term_rate}, not \eqref{eq:four_term_rate}, is the applicable bound. Realizing Corollary~\ref{cor:fixed_point_correction} in practice needs only a direct modification of Algorithm~\ref{alg:mlpiflnn} (replace the single ``second pass'' with a \texttt{repeat/until} on $\abs{\mathbf c_\sigma^{(m+1)}-\mathbf c_\sigma^{(m)}}<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GN}}$), but implementing it and checking $\EE(L_\mathcal T^2)<1$ numerically is left as the paper's principal open direction.

\section{Numerical experiments}\label{sec:numerics}
We evaluate the ML-PIFLNN on four benchmark problems, each activating a further component of the method. A deterministic fractional relaxation equation (Example~\ref{ex:ou}(i)) isolates the fractional feature layer. The fractional Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process (Example~\ref{ex:ou}(ii)) adds affine diffusion. Geometric Brownian motion (Example~\ref{ex:gbm}) adds state-dependent multiplicative diffusion and activates the stochastic operational matrix $\mathbf S_\alpha$. A square-root diffusion (Example~\ref{ex:sqrt}) breaks the affine structure and engages the Gauss--Newton solver, at the edge of what \S\ref{sec:convergence} covers. A non-linear trigonometric drift/diffusion (Example~\ref{ex:trig}) engages the Malliavin trace correction of \S\ref{sec:malliavin} in full.

All experiments were run on a Mac Studio (Apple M3 Ultra, 28-core CPU, 60-core GPU, 256~GB unified memory) running macOS 15.7.4.

For $\alpha = 1$, the reference solution uses a Milstein scheme on a mesh of $N = 65{,}536$ steps. For $\alpha \in (1/2, 1)$, we use the fast fractional Euler--Maruyama (fEM) method of \cite{zhang2023fast} on the same mesh. The network uses $N_c = 64$ Chebyshev collocation points \eqref{eq:chebyshev} and $R = 500$ sample paths per example, with $n$ denoting the M\"untz--Legendre feature-layer degree. Table~\ref{tab:numerical_parameters} lists the remaining algorithmic parameters, and Table~\ref{tab:model_parameters} lists the model coefficients ($\theta, \mu, \sigma, y_0$) used in each example.

We report two families of error. The trajectory errors
\begin{equation}\label{eq:strong_errors}
    \mathcal E_\infty = \sup_{t \in T} \frac 1R \sum_{i=1}^R \abs{y^{(i)}(t) - \hat y_n^{(i)}(t)}^2, \qquad
    \mathcal E_2 = \int_0^1 \frac 1R \sum_{i=1}^R \abs{y^{(i)}(t) - \hat y_n^{(i)}(t)}^2 \dd{t},
\end{equation}
where $y^{(i)}$ and $\hat y_n^{(i)}$ are the reference and ML-PIFLNN solutions for the $i$-th of $R$ sample paths, are mean-square quantities rather than strong errors, chosen to be directly comparable to the bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}. Tables~\ref{tab:ou_traj}, \ref{tab:gbm_traj}, and \ref{tab:sqrt_traj} additionally report $\mathcal E_\infty,\mathcal E_2$ with a resampled Monte Carlo standard error (not extended to Table~\ref{tab:trig_traj} or the discrete mean-error tables). The discrete mean errors $\mathcal E_{\mathcal L^2}^{\mathrm{mean}}$ and $\mathcal E_{\mathcal L^\infty}^{\mathrm{mean}}$ measure the deviation of the empirical mean $\bar y_R(t)$ from a reference mean trajectory: the closed form $\EE[y(t)] = y_0 E_\alpha(\mu t^\alpha)$ for the affine examples, and the empirical mean of the benchmark paths for the non-linear trigonometric example, where no closed form is available.

\begin{example}[Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process]\label{ex:ou}
    We consider the general linear Caputo stochastic fractional differential equation with mean-reverting drift and additive diffusion:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:ou}
        \DD y(t) = \theta(\mu - y(t)) + \sigma \sdv, \quad t \in T, \quad y(0) = y_0.
    \end{equation}

    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item \emph{Zero diffusion (deterministic validation).} Let $\mu = \sigma = 0$, $\theta = 1.0$, and $y_0 = 1.0$. Equation \eqref{eq:ou} reduces to the deterministic Caputo fractional relaxation equation
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:relaxation}
            \DD y(t) = -y(t), \quad y(0) = 1,
        \end{equation}
        whose exact solution is the one-parameter Mittag-Leffler function $y(t) = E_\alpha(-t^\alpha)$. With stochasticity absent, accuracy depends only on the exactness of the deterministic operational matrix $\mathbf P_\alpha(t)$, making this a pure validation of the fractional feature layer. The maximum absolute error and the integrated $\mathcal L^2$ error are reported in Tables \ref{tab:relax_linf} and \ref{tab:relax_l2}.

        \item \emph{Non-zero diffusion (stochastic fractional OU process).} Let $\theta = 0.3$, $\mu = 0.0$, $\sigma = 0.15$, and $y_0 = 1.0$. The drift $b(t, y) = -\theta y$ is linear and the diffusion $\sigma(t, y) = \sigma$ is state-independent, placing the process in the affine class, so the ML-PIFLNN bypasses iterative optimization and computes the coefficients via the pseudoinverse solve \eqref{eq:theta_star} across all $R = 500$ sample paths at once. Tables \ref{tab:ou_traj} and \ref{tab:ou_mean} report the trajectory and discrete mean errors, and Figure \ref{fig:qq_fou_b} gives a quantile--quantile comparison of the terminal distributions.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Square-root diffusion]\label{ex:sqrt}
    We consider a fractional stochastic process which features an expanding linear drift alongside a non-linear square-root diffusion, comparable to a non-mean-reverting Cox--Ingersoll--Ross process:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:sqrt_diffusion}
        \DD y(t) = \mu y(t) + \sigma (y(t))^{1/2} \sdv, \quad t \in T, \quad y(0) = y_0,
    \end{equation}
    with $\mu = 0.3$, $\sigma = 0.15$, and $y_0 = 1.0$. The non-linear diffusion $\sigma(t, y) = \sigma y^{1/2}$ breaks the affine structure, so the ML-PIFLNN switches to the damped Gauss--Newton collocation solver \eqref{eq:gn_update}, initialized from the linearized solution around $y_0$. Since $\sigma(t,y)=\sigma y^{1/2}$ is neither globally Lipschitz in $y$ (violating \ref{as:a2}) nor differentiable at $y=0$ (violating \ref{as:a4}), Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence} does not formally cover this example, and we present it as a test beyond the reach of the theory rather than a validated instance of it. Closing this gap rigorously would need a localization argument keeping $y$ bounded away from $0$, which we do not pursue here. Empirically, the fitted solution stays strictly positive throughout the test suite, although this is not a property guaranteed by the (unconstrained, positivity-agnostic) global polynomial fit and need not hold for other $\mu,\sigma,y_0$. Table \ref{tab:sqrt_traj} shows the same convergence rate is maintained despite the iterative solve, Table \ref{tab:sqrt_mean} gives the discrete mean errors across $\alpha$, and Figure \ref{fig:qq_sqrt} shows the terminal quantile--quantile comparison.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Non-linear trigonometric process]\label{ex:trig}
    To evaluate the ML-PIFLNN on a fully coupled non-linear problem requiring the explicit Malliavin trace (Skorokhod-vs-It\^o, not It\^o--Stratonovich) correction of \S\ref{sec:malliavin} in earnest, we consider the fractional stochastic differential equation with trigonometric drift and diffusion:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:trig}
        \DD y(t) = \mu\cos(y(t)) + \sigma\sin(y(t))\sdv, \quad t \in T, \quad y(0) = y_0,

Furthermore, the projection of the non-linear multiplicative noise onto the deterministic M\"untz--Legendre basis induces the anticipative bias identified in \S\ref{sec:malliavin}, so the explicit Malliavin trace correction \eqref{eq:trace_intro} is actively engaged in the second pass. Two convergence regimes appear across the examples. The deterministic case (Example~\ref{ex:ou}(i), Tables~\ref{tab:relax_linf}--\ref{tab:relax_l2}) converges spectrally, since the target $E_\alpha(-t^\alpha)$ is real-analytic and the operational matrix $\mathbf P_\alpha(t)$ is exact. The stochastic examples (Tables~\ref{tab:ou_traj}, \ref{tab:gbm_traj}, \ref{tab:sqrt_traj}, \ref{tab:trig_traj}) instead converge algebraically at order $1/2$ in $n$, matching the H\"older regularity of the underlying stochastic convolution and the $\delta_S(n)$ obstruction of Theorem~\ref{thm:convergence}: no global basis can approximate a Brownian-driven process faster than its own regularity allows. This confirms the theory rather than exposing a shortcoming, and situates the method as a mesh-free, globally evaluable alternative at a matched asymptotic rate rather than an improved one. A wall-clock comparison against fEM and Milstein at matched accuracy is not included in this study.
